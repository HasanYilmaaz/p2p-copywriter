# P2P Copy Formulas & Structures

How to use this file: gather the ingredients (sections 1-2), remember what the copy has to accomplish (section 3), then assemble the draft with the 6-Step Formula (section 4). Sections 6-7 are alternative recipes. After drafting, edit with `persuasion-filters.md`. For opening angles, see `proven-angles.md`.

---

## 1. Ingredients Before Recipes

The formulas below are recipes. A recipe only arranges ingredients; it does not create them. **The quality of the finished copy is set by the quality of the ingredients, not by the structure.** The same six steps filled with shallow material produce shallow copy. This is why research comes first and why the structure alone is worth little.

A cook follows recipes and can still produce very good work. A chef understands why each ingredient is there and can build new recipes. Start as a cook: follow the structure rigidly. Section 9 explains when and how to deviate.

**Never write from a blank page.** Before drafting, assemble an ingredient sheet for the audience. Drafting is then mostly selecting and connecting material that already exists, plus editing so it reads as one piece:

| Ingredient | What it is | Feeds which step |
|---|---|---|
| Angles | Hook ideas built from the research (see `proven-angles.md`) | Lead / Verify |
| Experiences | Things the reader has seen, heard, tried, or felt | Verify |
| External problems | The visible, surface problem | Verify (optional) |
| Internal problems | How the problem makes them feel: fear, shame, frustration | Validate |
| Philosophical problems + dualities | The deeper conflict; two opposing ideas | Pivot |
| Explanations / mechanisms | The "because" behind the conflict | Explanation |
| Benefits | What life looks like without the pain point | Benefit, CTA |
| CTA destination | Where the click goes (video, page, call) | CTA |

If the sheet is thin (no internal problem, no duality, no mechanism), stop and do research (`research-framework.md`, p2p-research skill) or ask the user. With a full sheet, several pieces can be written quickly by picking a different angle and recombining.

## 2. The 5 Basic Elements (Minimum Brief)

Collect these before writing anything:

1. **Target Customer** -- specific but not absurdly narrow; demographics plus behavior. The reader should think "that's me". Example: "women over 35 who have tried several diets".
2. **Desired Outcome** -- the specific result in their head. "Lose 3 kilos of belly fat", not "lose weight". "Make $10,000/month", not "make money".
3. **Benefit** -- what makes the outcome even more attractive, usually time or ease. "...in under 45 days", "...without previous experience".
4. **Pain Point** -- the biggest obstacle or frustration on the way to the outcome (starvation diets, giving up favorite foods, paying upfront). Naming it handles the objection before it is raised.
5. **Signature Solution** -- a unique name for the solution. Avoid the category word, because it invites comparison with everything else in the category: "protocol", "system", "method", "challenge" instead of "diet" or "course".

## 3. What Every Piece Must Accomplish

Work backward from the goal:

- The goal is always **action** (a click, a purchase, a booked call).
- Nobody acts without **desire** for what the action gives them.
- Desire cannot be built on a topic they have no **interest** in.
- Interest cannot be created without their **attention**.

So every piece, short or long, must win attention, interest, desire, and action. The stages may overlap (desire for the click can start building in the middle of the copy), but all four must be accomplished by the piece as a whole.

### The lead is the highest-leverage part

The lead is the first few paragraphs of long copy, the first 3-4 lines of an email, the first two sentences of an ad, the first ~30 seconds of a video.

- Most of the audience loss happens there. In the instructor's video data, a weak intro kept roughly 58% of viewers at the 30-second mark; a strong one kept about 77%. After that point both curves are almost flat: people who get through the opening mostly stay to the end.
- Therefore the same body with a better opening puts far more people in front of the CTA. Moving from about 50% to 75% retention is 50% more people reaching the CTA, with nothing else changed. No edit deeper in the copy has that leverage, and in a long piece it is hard to even know which later argument to change.
- **Spend most of the writing time on the opening.** Choose the angle deliberately, draft more than one lead, and keep the strongest.
- When reviewing, judge the lead first. If it is weak, the rest barely matters because few people will reach it.

## 4. The 6-Step Formula (Primary Structure)

Use this for emails, ads, and any short-to-medium copy. It is preferred over the alternatives because it is built on the internal problem, the philosophical problem, and a duality, so it speaks at an emotional level, and because each step makes it obvious what to write next.

**Why it starts where it does.** Everyone sits on a pyramid of experiences, assumptions, conclusions, and beliefs, inside a self-sealing logic bubble. A message is first tested for internal coherence: does it match what I have lived and believe? Only messages that pass are then tested for external validity: is it actually true in the world? A message that fails the first test is discarded without consideration. The formula therefore opens inside the reader's experience and only afterwards introduces something new and proves it.

| # | Step | Job | Typical length (email) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Verify | Pass the bubble, win attention | 1-2 sentences |
| 2 | Validate internal problem | Emotional connection, "they get me", interest | 1 sentence to a short paragraph |
| 3 | Pivot to philosophical problem | Create conflict with a duality; the reader's own story starts | 2-4 sentences |
| 4 | Explanation | Logical "because"; passes the external validity test | 1-3 sentences |
| 5 | Benefit | Make the pending decision more attractive | 1-2 sentences |
| 6 | Call to action | Say exactly what to do next | 1-3 sentences |

### Step 1: Verify

- **Do:** open with something the reader has experienced, seen, heard, or tried. It can be the external problem, but does not have to be. A question ("Do you often...?") or a described moment both work.
- **Why:** it has internal coherence, so it gets through the bubble. The reader also assumes you see reality through the same lens, so you must understand how they think and feel. Recognition ("yes, that happens to me") is what captures attention.
- **Better still:** include a duality already in the opening.
- **Length:** one or two sentences in an email, where space and patience are short. Longer formats can take several paragraphs.
- **Mistakes:** opening with the product, with your own claim or conclusion, or with something they have never experienced. All of these sit outside the bubble.

### Step 2: Validate the Internal Problem

- **Do:** describe how the situation makes them feel, using the internal problem from research: the fear, frustration, shame, the thing they avoid because of it.
- **Why:** this is communication at the emotional level. When the reader feels understood, a bond forms and they want to hear the rest. This step turns attention into interest.
- **How:** take the feeling straight from the research and state it plainly. Nothing new needs to be invented.
- **Mistake:** restating the external problem in different words. This step is about the feeling, not the fact.

### Step 3: Pivot to the Philosophical Problem (Duality)

- **Do:** use the internal problem as the bridge to the deeper problem, and express it as a duality. A philosophical problem usually contains one already; if it does not, add one.
- **Why:** a duality creates conflict, conflict starts a story in the reader's head, and that story produces the decision. Done well, the reader has decided to act before the CTA asks, so the CTA feels smooth.
- **Strongest form, the flip:** the thing they believe is helping them is the thing holding them back. Example: they tried the strict diets, and the strict diets are exactly why the weight came back.
- **Other form, the open question:** "We were taught hunger means you need food. What if the hunger you feel isn't for food?" The unanswered "then what is it?" pulls toward the click.
- The duality used here may differ from the main one found in research. Choose the one that fits the angle of this piece.
- **Mistake:** announcing the conclusion ("so you need my program"). Seed the conflict and let the reader resolve it.

### Step 4: Explanation

- **Do:** say why the conflict is true: "this happens because of X". Name the mechanism from research (for example: strict diets slow the metabolism and switch on hunger hormones).
- **Why:** decisions are made emotionally and justified logically. Step 3 handled the emotion; the reader now needs an argument to justify the decision to themselves. This is also the external validity check: having let the idea in, they ask whether it holds in the real world.
- **How:** short and plain. A cause, optionally one piece of proof. Do not give away the full solution; the destination behind the CTA delivers that.

### Step 5: Benefit

- **Do:** add a benefit on top, ideally the removal of the pain point ("you never have to go on a starvation diet again").
- **Why:** by now the reader is ready to decide. The benefit makes saying yes more attractive.
- **How:** pull from the benefit list; one or two lines.

### Step 6: Call to Action

- **Do:** tell them exactly what to do next. Every message ends with a CTA, without exception.
- **Sell the click, not the product.** In an email or ad the action is usually "watch this video" or "read this page". Let the page or video do the selling. Pitching the product directly inside an email is reserved for rare, specific promotions.
- **Single or double CTA:** one plain line with the link is often enough. To deepen it, add another benefit or duality after the first link, then repeat the link.
- **Close the loop:** end on the image or topic the piece opened with. If it opened with dreading photos, it ends with photos becoming enjoyable again. This makes the piece feel complete.

### Rules that apply across all six steps

- **Open each new segment with a short connecting sentence** ("And worse yet,", "Truth is,", "Here's why.", "The fact is,"). It creates continuity, breaks up longer passages into peaks and valleys, and keeps people reading. Library in section 10.
- **Match length to the size of the ask.** A click from people who already know you needs little convincing, so one sentence per step can be enough. Colder audiences and bigger asks need more. Short is not automatically better and neither is long.
- **Plug and play.** Each step should be filled from the ingredient sheet. If a step requires inventing something, the research is missing a piece.

## 5. Worked Examples (6-Step)

Illustrative emails for a weight-loss audience (women over 35). The CTA sells a click to a video.

### Example A: full-length email (angle: dreading photos)

| Step | Copy |
|---|---|
| Verify | Do you get a knot in your stomach when someone pulls out a camera? Like you already know you won't like the person in the picture. |
| Validate | **And worse yet,** it doesn't end when the photo is taken. It follows you around. You start checking how you look, dodging the camera when you can, and every picture feels like a reminder of how far you are from where you want to be. |
| Pivot | **Truth is,** most people who feel this way have already tried to fix it. Low-carb. Keto. Cutting calories. And that's exactly the problem. Strict diets make you gain even more weight in the long run. |
| Explanation | **Here's why.** They slow your metabolism and switch on your hunger hormones, which makes losing weight even harder. |
| Benefit | That's why, once you understand how those hormones work, you never have to starve yourself again. |
| CTA | **The fact is,** you don't have to feel hungry and deprived to lose weight. I explain how in this video: [link]. Once you apply it, food becomes a tool instead of an enemy, and photos become something you enjoy again. If that's what you want, watch it now: [link] |

What to notice: the opening is a lived moment, not a claim. Validate adds the feeling. The pivot contains two dualities (tried and failed; the cure is the cause). The CTA adds a second duality (tool vs enemy), closes the photo loop, and repeats the link.

### Example B: short email (angle: hungry after meals)

| Step | Copy |
|---|---|
| Verify | Do you often feel hungry even after you've just eaten? |
| Validate | Like no matter how much you eat, it's never enough? |
| Pivot | We've all been taught that hunger means you need food. But what if the hunger you feel isn't for food? |
| Explanation | Feeling hungry right after a meal can be a sign of a hormone imbalance. It slows your metabolism and keeps your hunger signals switched on, even when you're full. |
| Bridge to click | What causes it? I recorded a video on why so many women over 35 run into this, and how to tell if you're one of them. (Hint: it has to do with the strict diets everyone recommends.) |
| Benefit | The fact is, you can lose weight without feeling hungry all day. |
| CTA | If you want to see how, here's the video: [link] |

What to notice: one sentence each for Verify and Validate is enough for a warm list. The open question in the pivot creates the urge to click. A single one-line CTA does the job.

## 6. PWAS (Problem - Why - Agitate - Solve)

A variation of the well-known Problem-Agitate-Solve with a "Why" step added so the problem is explained before it is intensified.

1. **Problem** -- lead with one specific problem. Similar to Verify, but here it must be a problem.
   > "Counting calories and following strict diets? That may be exactly why the weight won't come off."
2. **Why** -- explain why it is a problem: what causes it, or what it causes in their life. Use the mechanism from research. Short self-asked questions keep it moving ("How is that possible?", "What are those?").
3. **Agitate** -- make them feel the pain. A good copywriter does not only stab with the problem but twists the knife. The pain felt now is what creates desire for the solution a moment later. Typical opener: "And the worst part is...".
   > "If this imbalance is what you're dealing with, it doesn't matter how hard you try. You'll most likely fail."
4. **Solve** -- present the way out and the CTA. "Solution" means the next step appropriate to the medium (in an email: "watch this video to find out if this is affecting you"), not necessarily the product. Add a benefit and close the loop on the opening problem.

## 7. BAB (Before - After - Bridge)

The structure is itself a duality: before vs after, problem vs desire, rock bottom vs top of the mountain. Use it when the contrast between the two states is the strongest material available.

1. **Before** -- here is what your problem looks like today.
2. **After** -- here is what the world looks like once it is solved.
3. **Bridge** -- here is how you get there: the vehicle from before to after, plus the CTA.

**Caution.** BAB pushes the writer toward salesman language and toward pitching the product too hard. Openers like "Are you tired of...", "Have you ever imagined...", "Imagine..." are overused; readers have learned that a sales pitch follows, and their guard goes up. Vary the phrasing, keep the bridge honest, and do not use the literal word "bridge". The same before/after effect can be achieved inside the 6-Step Formula with less sales pressure.

## 8. Choosing a Structure

| Situation | Use |
|---|---|
| Default for emails, ads, short-to-medium copy | **6-Step Formula** |
| The research is thin on internal/philosophical depth, or the user asks for a problem-led piece | PWAS |
| The transformation contrast is the main asset, or the user asks for it | BAB (watch the tone) |
| Long-form sales video or letter | `longform-canvas.md` |

PWAS and BAB lead with shallower problems and are less clear about what comes next, which is why they are alternatives and not the default. They still rely on dualities; any structure does.

## 9. From Cook to Chef

The structures are not magic sequences. Each exists to move the reader through attention, interest, desire, and action while starting inside their bubble, reaching emotion before logic, and creating a conflict they resolve themselves. Once those principles are understood, steps can be merged, reordered, or extended (a longer Verify for cold traffic, a duality inside the opening line, the CTA seeded during the pivot). Deviate only when you can say which principle the change serves. If you cannot, follow the recipe.

## 10. Connecting Words Library

Use at the start of a new segment and between ideas. There are hundreds; these are starting points.

- "And worse yet..." / "And the worst part is..."
- "Truth is..." / "Here's the truth."
- "Here's why." / "That's why..."
- "The fact is..." / "Here's the fact of the matter."
- "Here's the thing..."
- "How is that possible?" / "What causes that, you may ask?"
- "But here's what most people don't realize..."
- "In other words..." / "Put simply..."
- "But it doesn't have to be that way."
- "Here's the good news."

## 11. Prompts for Drafting With AI

Structure prompts only work well in the same conversation that already holds the audience research (problems, desires, angles), because the model can then draw on those ingredients. Skeleton:

```
Write an email for this target audience using one of the angles from
our previous messages, following this structure:
[Step name]: [one-line instruction]
... (one line per step of the chosen structure)
Make it punchy and combine short sentences with long sentences.
```

Step lines for each structure:
- **6-Step:** Verify (something they have experienced) / Validate (how it makes them feel) / Pivot (deeper problem, with a duality) / Explain (why it happens) / Benefit / Call to action.
- **PWAS:** Problem (lead with a problem) / Why (why it is a problem and what causes it) / Agitate (make them feel the pain) / Solve (give the solution).
- **BAB:** Before (what the problem looks like) / After (what the world looks like once solved) / Bridge (how to get there).

Then review the output before using it:
- Vocabulary is usually too complex. Follow up with: "Rewrite it so a 10-year-old would understand."
- It tends to pitch the product inside the email. Redirect the CTA to the click.
- Harvest it. Drafts often contain new dualities and analogies worth adding to the ingredient sheet (for example "deprivation vs temptation", "are you in control of food, or is food in control of you?", "trapped in a maze with no way out"). An analogy that carries a duality is especially useful.
- Do not use AI as a crutch. It is strongest at surfacing problems and ideas; refine the lead and the angle yourself.

## 12. Fill-in-the-Blanks Short VSL Template (3-5 min)

```
HOOK: "If you are [TARGET CUSTOMER] who wants to [DESIRED OUTCOME]
but [PAIN POINT], watch out."

INTRODUCTION: "Hi, my name is [NAME] and if you want to
[DESIRED OUTCOME + BENEFIT] without [PAIN POINT], pay close attention."

LIMITING BELIEF: "Many [TARGET CUSTOMERS] currently suffer from the idea
that [LIMITING BELIEF]. This idea leads to [CONSEQUENCES]."

VALIDATION: "And as a result, most feel like [INTERNAL FRUSTRATION].
But it doesn't have to be that way."

SOLUTION TEASE (not the product yet): "If you want to
[DESIRED OUTCOME + BENEFIT], all you need is [SOLUTION CONCEPT]."

THROW ROCKS: "I could tease you for 50 minutes like everyone else in
this industry, but I'd rather be upfront with you."

SIGNATURE SOLUTION: "Let me introduce [NAME]. It's a
[system/protocol/program] that lets you [DESIRED OUTCOME]
without [PAIN POINT]."

REINFORCE: repeat desired outcome + pain point removal.

SOCIAL PROOF: "This is the same system [CLIENT] used to
[SPECIFIC RESULT] in [TIMEFRAME]."

CTA: "If you want [help implementing this] during the next
[TIMEFRAME], [book a call / buy / click]."
```

For full long-form structure see `longform-canvas.md`.

## 13. The 4 Big Niches

Money (business, career, side income), Relationships (dating, marriage), Health (fitness, weight loss, anti-aging), Hobbies (pets, photography, sports, survival). In every niche, think problem-first, not product-first.
