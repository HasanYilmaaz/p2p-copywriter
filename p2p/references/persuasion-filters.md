# P2P Persuasion Filters

A first draft is a draft, not a product. It does not need to be good; editing is what turns it into high-converting copy. The six filters below are the editing passes, in the order they are run. Each pass is a full re-read with one question in mind. The purpose is to confirm the copy accomplishes what it must, remove what is unnecessary, and improve what is not there yet.

Sections: the six filters (1-6), the additional P2P depth checks (7-8), the editing procedure, and the scoring rubric used by the p2p-review skill.

---

## Filter 1: AIDA, Checked in Reverse

**Question:** does the piece as a whole win attention, raise interest, build desire, and get action?

**How to apply.** Reverse-engineer the copy from the end:

1. **Action** -- find the CTA. Is it there, is it one clear instruction, and would a reader know exactly what to do next?
2. **Desire** -- look at what comes before the CTA. Has the copy made them want what the action gives them?
3. **Interest** -- before that: is there a reason to care and keep reading (feeling understood, an open question, a conflict)?
4. **Attention** -- finally the opening lines: would the target reader stop for this?

**Why reverse.** Each stage depends on the one before it. Starting from the action makes it easy to see where the chain breaks: a clear CTA with no desire behind it, or desire built on an opening nobody will read.

**Judgment notes.**
- The four stages do not need to sit in four separate blocks in strict order. Desire for the click can start building during the pivot; the CTA is not the only place that drives action. What matters is that the complete piece accomplishes all four.
- Weight the attention check most heavily. The opening lines decide how many people see everything else (see `copy-formulas.md`, section 3). A piece with a weak lead fails this filter even if the rest is strong.

**Fix:** add or sharpen the missing stage. No CTA: add one. Weak opening: replace it with something the reader has experienced, or a stronger angle (`proven-angles.md`).

## Filter 2: The 10-Year-Old Test

**Question:** is this written so a 10-year-old would understand it?

**How to apply.** Re-read and mark:
- words that are too complex or too "written" (replace with the everyday word);
- sentences whose structure is unclear (restructure or shorten);
- concepts that are not explained clearly enough for a child to follow (explain them differently, or with a comparison).

**Why.** The first job of copy is to be understood. If the reader has to stop and think about what a sentence means, the copy has failed at that point. Dumbing copy down is a good thing: the simpler it is, the more people understand it, and people who do not understand do not act.

**Judgment notes.**
- A technical term can stay if it is the mechanism and is explained immediately in plain words ("hunger hormones", then what they do).
- AI-generated drafts fail this filter most often: ornate vocabulary, literary phrasing. Rewrite them in plain speech.

| Too complex | Plain |
|---|---|
| "exuding confidence and vivaciousness" | "looking happy and sure of yourself" |
| "cultivating a mindful relationship with food" | "enjoying your food without feeling guilty" |
| "it permeates every facet of your life" | "it follows you everywhere" |

## Filter 3: Length and Variety (Peaks and Valleys)

**Question:** is any sentence or paragraph longer than it needs to be, and could it be cut or shortened without losing the meaning of that part?

**How to apply.**
1. Cut first. Remove sentences and paragraphs the piece does not need. If the meaning survives without it, it goes.
2. Shorten what remains where possible.
3. Look for runs of long sentences. A long sentence is sometimes necessary; several in a row is the problem. This usually appears in explanations, where the writer gets too detailed.
4. Break runs up: split the idea into shorter sentences, or, if the long sentences are needed, put a short connecting sentence between them.

**Why.** Walls of text lose readers before they start. The copy should look like a mountain range viewed sideways, with peaks and valleys: long lines mixed with short ones. The visual shape matters as well as the reading rhythm.

## Filter 4: Abstract vs Concrete

**Question:** are there terms, sentences, or claims that are too abstract and could be made concrete and tangible?

**How to apply.** Re-read looking only for abstract language. For each instance ask: if 100 people read this, would they all picture the same thing? If not, decide whether to make it concrete.

| Abstract | Concrete |
|---|---|
| "Achieve financial freedom" | "Have $10,436.26 land in your bank account every month" |
| "Feel confident in social situations" | "Feel at ease at your friend's backyard barbecue next weekend" |
| "Signs of aging" | "Wrinkles around your eyes" |
| "Be confident at work" | "Lead the Monday meeting knowing people are listening to your ideas" |
| "Feel better about how you look" | "Like the person you see in the group photo" |

**Judgment notes.**
- Not every abstract term has to go. A general term such as "weight loss" is acceptable when it is followed by a concrete benefit or claim that explains what it means in this case ("lose weight" followed by "see the number on the scale go down without starving yourself").
- The failure is accumulation: many abstract terms with nothing tangible near them. Make sure concrete, tangible claims run through the whole piece.

## Filter 5: Connecting Words

**Question:** are there enough connecting words between ideas and between paragraphs?

**How to apply.** Check every point where the copy moves to a new idea or segment. It is easy to get absorbed in the ideas and forget to join them. Add a short connector where two ideas simply sit next to each other: "Here's the truth.", "The fact is,", "Here's why.", "And worse yet,", "Here's the fact of the matter." (Library in `copy-formulas.md`, section 10.)

**Why.** Connectors make the copy flow, break up the page visually, and measurably improve how many people read to the end.

**Judgment notes.** A connector should join two ideas, not decorate one. Vary them; the same connector three times in a short email reads as a tic.

## Filter 6: One-Sentence Persuasion (Blair Warren)

> "People will do anything for those who encourage their dreams, justify their failures, allay their fears, confirm their suspicions, and help them throw rocks at their enemies."

**Question:** which of the five elements does the copy contain?

1. **Encourage their dreams.** Tell them the goal is achievable for them. This builds rapport because it says they are capable. Pattern: "It is absolutely possible for you to [outcome]."
2. **Justify their failures.** Give an outside reason for past failure. Pattern: "The reason it hasn't worked, every time you tried, is [cause]. It's not your fault." Taking pressure off the reader's shoulders is one of the most powerful moves available. It can be stated, or implied by naming the outside cause and letting the reader conclude it.
3. **Allay their fears.** Reduce the fear that it will not work for them or will be painful. This usually follows from the first two: once the failure has a cause and the goal is possible, the fear eases.
4. **Confirm their suspicions.** People hold suspicions they want to be true (for example, that the diets they hate are not actually necessary). Confirm one and they are glad to believe you, because they wanted to believe it already.
5. **Help them throw rocks at their enemies.** Create a common enemy. Nothing bonds people faster: a shared enemy turns "me against the world" into "us against the world" and gives a sense of shared mission. The enemy does not have to be a person or a villain. It can be a method, an industry habit, a limitation, or the obstacle itself.

**How to apply.** Tick off the elements. One well-built passage can cover several at once. Example covering 1, 2, and 3: "You can lose this weight. The reason it hasn't worked is the diets you were told to follow. Change that, and it works for you too." Short copy does not need five separate passages, but the more elements present, the more persuasive the piece. If "justify their failures" or a common enemy is missing, add it first; those two carry the most weight.

**Guardrail.** Choose enemies that are ideas, methods, or obstacles. Do not target or defame a real, named person or company.

## Additional P2P Checks

These go beyond the six filters and come from the method's foundations.

### 7. Duality and conflict
- Is there at least one clear duality (two opposing ideas) in the copy?
- Does it create a conflict the reader resolves themselves, or does the copy hand them the conclusion?
- Strongest form: the flip (what they think helps is what holds them back).

### 8. Problem depth and structure
- Does the opening start inside the reader's experience (Verify)?
- Does the copy reach the internal problem (how it feels) and the philosophical problem, or stay on the surface?
- Is there a logical explanation after the emotional part?
- Does it lead with the problem, with the product as the vehicle and not the opening topic?
- Is "you" used throughout, as a one-to-one conversation?
- Does the ending close the loop opened at the start?
- Does the signature solution avoid the category word?

## Editing Procedure (Self-Edit After a First Draft)

1. Finish the draft before editing. Do not polish while drafting.
2. Run filters 1 to 6 in order, one full read per filter. Fix as you go.
3. Run checks 7 and 8.
4. Re-read the opening lines one last time. If only one thing can be improved, improve those.
5. Re-run filter 2 at the end; fixes made in later passes often reintroduce complex wording.

## Scoring Rubric (for p2p-review)

Score each dimension 1-10 using the anchors. Quote the line that justifies each score.

| Dimension | Low (1-3) | Mid (4-7) | High (8-10) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. AIDA** | No CTA, or the opening gives the target reader no reason to stop. A stage is missing. | All four stages present but one is weak: generic opening, desire asserted and not built, or a vague/multiple CTA. | Opening stops the right reader in the first lines; interest and desire build; one clear CTA telling them exactly what to do. |
| **2. Simplicity** | Jargon, ornate vocabulary, tangled sentences; the reader must re-read to understand. | Mostly plain, with several complex words or one or two unclear sentences or concepts. | A 10-year-old could follow every sentence; any technical term is explained at once. |
| **3. Length & variety** | Walls of text; runs of long sentences; removable padding. | Readable, but with some padding or a stretch of same-length sentences. | Nothing left to cut; long and short alternate; the page shows peaks and valleys. |
| **4. Concrete vs abstract** | Claims are mostly abstract ("transform your life", "reach your goals"); nothing to picture. | Mix: some tangible images, several abstract claims left unsupported. | Key claims are tangible and specific; any abstract term is followed by a concrete one. |
| **5. Connecting words** | Ideas sit side by side with no transitions; choppy or disjointed. | Some connectors; a few abrupt jumps between segments, or the same connector overused. | Each change of idea or segment is bridged by a short, varied connector; reads in one flow. |
| **6. One-sentence persuasion** | None or one of the five elements. | Two or three elements; blame still rests on the reader or there is no common enemy. | Four or five elements, including justified failure and a common enemy, worked in naturally. |
| **7. Duality & conflict** | No duality; the copy only describes or asserts. | A duality exists but is weak, buried, or the conclusion is handed over. | A clear duality (ideally a flip) creates a conflict the reader resolves in favor of acting. |
| **8. Problem depth & structure** | Opens with the product or the writer's claim; external problem only; no explanation. | Starts with the problem and touches feelings, but no philosophical level, or emotion without the logical "because". | Opens inside the reader's experience, reaches internal and philosophical problem, explains why, product is the vehicle, loop closed. |

**Total: /80.** 70+: excellent, polish only. 50-69: sound but needs targeted work. Below 50: major revision or a rewrite from the structure.

**Lead rule.** If the opening lines fail (dimension 1 attention, dimension 8 opening), flag this as the first priority regardless of the total. A high total with a weak lead still describes copy that most people will not read.

Scores are a diagnostic aid, not a measurement. The findings and the rewrite are what the user needs.
