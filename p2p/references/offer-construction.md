# P2P Offer Construction (Launch Canvas Step 4)

How to map an offer into pillars, seed it through the whole promotion, and present it in the pitch: pillars -> tiered results -> price -> reasons to respond -> external scenario -> risk reversal.

Read with `longform-canvas.md` (the canvas this step belongs to).

Source note: this file follows the course's Offer lesson, which describes launch-event pitches. `vsl-breakdown.md` shows how one evergreen VSL actually executed its pitch; it adds a "two options" transition, modules presented as outcomes, a price tease before the drop, and a re-stack after the price, and that particular video has no guarantee section. Use this file to design the offer and that one to sequence a single-video pitch. Where they differ, pick what fits the user's real offer.

---

## 1. Why the Offer Is Mapped Before the Argument

Most long-form copy is written as two acts: "content" for 75-80% of the running time, then a hard turn into "sales". That turn catches the audience off guard. They were not expecting a pitch, they feel tricked, and they resist.

The goal is the opposite reaction: *"That's exactly what I need -- how did he know?"*

You get it by knowing the offer before you design the chain of beliefs, and creating the need for each part of it from the first minute. People decide emotionally and justify with logic, and they need time for that: the conflict has to be planted early so they can build their own story and sell themselves before the solution appears. If the need is only raised at the end, they are not ready, and the pitch irritates them.

So the order is: thesis -> offer pillars -> chain of beliefs -> script. Pricing and urgency details can be settled later; the pillars cannot.

---

## 2. Pillars, Not Features or Benefits

"Sell benefits, not features" is true but one-dimensional. A pillar is a level above both: **something a person must have in order to become the Top of the Mountain**. The offer then exists to fill those gaps.

### Procedure

1. List everything the buyer actually receives (course, calls, coaching, community, bonuses, software...).
2. Re-read the marketing thesis and the Top of the Mountain identity.
3. Ask: **"What does a person need in order to become [Top of Mountain]?"** Answer in 3-5 plain nouns.
4. Group the deliverables under those nouns. One deliverable can serve two pillars; several deliverables can sit under one.
5. For each pillar, write the narrative that makes the need obvious: a logical argument, an analogy, or an appeal to the prospect's own experience.
6. Feed the pillars into the chain of beliefs: one of the last links is always "to become [Top of Mountain] you need [pillars]".
7. Check that every pillar gets seeded somewhere before the pitch.

### Worked examples

**A. Identity-based pillars** (thesis: freedom -> top 1% -> become a "New Renaissance Man")

| Pillar | Offer component | Narrative that creates the need |
|--------|-----------------|---------------------------------|
| Knowledge + a business | Core course | To be in the top 1% you must know what the 99% don't; and nearly everyone in the top 1% owns a business |
| Community | Student community | Not "networking": being surrounded by people doing the work every day. You keep up less from inspiration than from the shame of being the one who doesn't |
| Accountability | Expert coaches, 1-on-1 coaches, peers, alumni | Safety nets that stop you falling through the cracks; "us against the world" instead of "me against the world" |
| Self-improvement | Partner bonus course | Money alone does not make a top 1% man; you must be on your game mentally and physically |

The Top of the Mountain is *defined* as the person who has all four. Once the audience wants to be that person, they already know what they are missing.

**B. Analogy-based pillars** (single course sold in a one-week event). The whole offer was framed with a gym analogy:
- **A plan** -- nobody gets in shape without a structured programme. One episode taught the plan step by step; each step silently matched one phase of the course. The pitch then revealed: "what I showed you in one hour is in the programme in fifty".
- **Accountability** -- in a gym there are veterans (experts), people who completed the same programme (alumni), and people starting with you (peers).
- **Intensity** -- see section 3.

**C. Abstracting a large bundle** (subscription with several courses, community, calls, platform). One pillar per course would be confusing. Instead the whole bundle was abstracted into one idea, **access**, with three pillars: access to the right opportunities, the right knowledge, the right people. The promotion then seeded a single narrative: "the difference between you and successful people is their level of access".

Rule: when an offer has many components, look for the one concept that covers them and split it into at most 3-5 pillars.

### Tips for pillar narratives

- Use what buyers have told you. If surveys show students feel alone because nobody around them shares their goals, the community/peer pillar is a major selling point: seed it throughout.
- Moving away from pain motivates more strongly than moving toward gain. Frame at least one pillar around what it protects them from.
- A pillar narrative usually needs a line of logic the audience can agree with immediately ("If you had access to the right knowledge at the right time, would your odds be higher?").

## 3. Designing Bonuses as Pillars

A bonus is strongest when it IS a pillar (or fills a gap in one), not an extra thrown on top.

- **Damaging admission -> bonus.** State an honest weakness of the main product, then solve it with the bonus. "The programme is extremely detailed, which means it's fifty hours long. If you want to move fast, that's a problem. So..." -> a short live fast-track series. The admission makes everything else more believable, and the bonus now has a reason to exist.
- **Give the bonus its own pillar narrative.** The fast-track series was sold as *intensity*: intense starts create disruption, and disruption creates action; elite units and diamonds are formed under intense conditions. That narrative was seeded long before the pitch.
- **Fill a missing pillar from outside.** "My programme doesn't cover this pillar, so I bought the rights to someone else's course and you get it at no extra charge." Naming the gap yourself is another damaging admission, and the audience values getting something they would not otherwise have.
- A new bonus does not need to be a finished product at launch (e.g. live sessions scheduled after the cart closes), as long as it is real and will be delivered.

## 4. The Pitch, in Order

### 4.1 Transition: recap the chain, then fill the gaps

The pitch opens by restating what the audience has already accepted, then offering to supply it:

```
So you already understand that to get [real desire], you have to [new opportunity].
And the best way to do that is to become [Top of Mountain].
For that you need [pillar 1], [pillar 2], [pillar 3] and [pillar 4].
That is what I want to give you today.

[Product] gives you [pillar 1]. Here's what's inside... (how it fills the gap)
For [pillar 2], you get...
[Pillar 4] is honestly something [product] didn't cover. So I... (damaging admission -> bonus)
```

Present each component as the fulfilment of a need the audience already feels, never as a list of features.

### 4.2 Proof through a tiered results system

Testimonials shown as a block lose attention and feel like a hard sell. Instead give them a context that adds to the narrative: a named ladder of outcomes that the community itself uses.

Example ladder for a client-acquisition programme:
- Tier 1 (small clients, $1-3K/month): a couple of these already replace an income; "students land these daily".
- Tier 2 (mid clients, $3-8K/month): one can let you quit your job.
- Tier 3 (large clients, $8K+/month): one alone is a six-figure year; the "north star" of the community.

Then show student results under each tier. This does three jobs at once: it proves the pillars work, it sells the community and its culture, and it gives prospects a ladder to imagine climbing.

To adapt: define 3 named milestones with concrete thresholds, explain why the top threshold matters, attach real results to each. Only use testimonials the user supplies.

### 4.3 Price presentation (breakdown attributed to Todd Brown)

Five beats, in this order:

1. **Regular price, with a reason.** The normal price must make sense on its own; an unexplained "usually $3,000" is not believed. Tie it to a principle. Example: the company's mission is an education with many times the income impact of college at a hundredth of the cost; a hundredth of a typical degree is still out of reach for many, so the regular price was set lower.
2. **Value stack.** Add up what the components are worth separately (main product + bonus at its own future price = package total).
3. **Discount, with a reason.** Why today's price is lower: a thank-you for following the event, a launch, a one-time offer. A discount without a rationale looks like a trick.
4. **Final price.** State it plainly.
5. **Price trivialisation.** Reduce it to a small daily amount ("spread over a year, that's less than a coffee a day").

### 4.4 Reasons to respond: urgency and scarcity

If people can decide tomorrow, they will, and by tomorrow the emotions you raised have cooled. Getting the decision today roughly doubles sales compared with leaving it open. Two separate tools:

- **Urgency** = a deadline. After this date the offer is gone.
- **Scarcity** = something they lose if they wait. Limited places, or (preferred) a **fast-action bonus** for the first N buyers or first hours.

What the instructor's own launches showed (three one-week cart-open periods):

| Setup | Sales pattern |
|-------|---------------|
| Deadline only, no fast-action bonus | A handful of sales on day one, slow climb, most sales on the last day |
| Fast-action bonus that was only *part* of one pillar, and only meant waiting a few weeks | Big first-day spike plus a second spike at close; some buyers lost in between |
| Fast-action bonus perceived as an entire pillar | Roughly 80% of sales on day one, most of them in the first hour, while viewers were still watching |

Lesson: **the strength of a fast-action bonus depends on how much of a pillar the buyer believes they will miss.** Make it a meaningful piece of the offer (priority access, a VIP group, an extra programme), not a trinket. Capture buyers while they are hot; do not rely on the final day.

All urgency and scarcity must be real. Do not imply a limit that does not exist; if a limit applies to only part of the offer, say so clearly.

### 4.5 External scenario

After the internal reasons to act, restate the macro reason: something bigger than the prospect that will happen whether or not they act, and will hurt them if they don't. This is the Big Idea's external urgency brought back at the close, expressed as the duality:

```
[The change] is coming either way.
If you do nothing, you end up [Rock Bottom].
If you act now, you still have the chance to become [Top of Mountain].
```

### 4.6 Risk reversal

End with the guarantee. Move all the risk from the buyer to the seller.

- **Unconditional guarantee.** A fixed period, any reason at all ("even if you don't like the colour of the play button"), full refund, fast, no questions.
- **Reframe the decision.** With a guarantee they are not deciding today; they are deciding to look inside. There is no financial risk.
- **Confidence framing.** "I'd rather take all the risk and let you experience it than keep telling you how good it is. The product will do the selling."
- **Conditional guarantee (optional, on top).** If they do the specified work (a concrete, measurable amount) and still get no result, refund plus extra for the wasted time.

Only write guarantee terms the user confirms they will honour.

### 4.7 Close

Restate the full value proposition (pillars, what they get, price, bonus, guarantee), give the call to action, and end. Prefer a single call to action; for a high-ticket offer a secondary "book a call" route for people who cannot pay in one go is acceptable (see `vsl-breakdown.md`).

## 5. Pitch Skeleton

```
[TRANSITION]   Recap accepted beliefs -> "you need [pillars]" -> "that's what I'm giving you"
[PILLARS]      Component by component, each as the answer to a seeded need
               (include damaging admission -> bonus)
[PROOF]        Tiered results system with student outcomes
[PRICE]        Regular price + reason -> value stack -> discount + reason -> final price -> per-day
[RESPOND NOW]  Deadline (urgency) + fast-action bonus (scarcity)
[EXTERNAL]     The change is coming either way: Rock Bottom vs. Top of Mountain
[GUARANTEE]    Unconditional (+ conditional) -> "no decision today, no risk"
[CLOSE]        Restate value proposition -> CTA
```

## 6. Offer Checklist

- [ ] Every pillar answers "what do they need to become [Top of Mountain]?"
- [ ] 3-5 pillars; a large bundle has been abstracted under one concept
- [ ] Each pillar has a narrative, and that narrative appears before the pitch
- [ ] The chain of beliefs contains "you need [pillars]" just before the offer
- [ ] At least one honest damaging admission
- [ ] Bonuses fill real gaps and are framed as pillars
- [ ] Testimonials sit inside a context (tier system), not in a block
- [ ] Regular price and discount each have a stated reason
- [ ] Price is trivialised to a small daily figure
- [ ] Real deadline AND a fast-action bonus that matters
- [ ] External scenario restates the duality
- [ ] Guarantee removes the risk and removes the need to "decide today"
- [ ] No invented prices, limits, testimonials or guarantee terms
