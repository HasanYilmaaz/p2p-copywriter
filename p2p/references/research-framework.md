# P2P Research Framework

Research is where P2P copy is actually written. The structures stay the same; the raw material you extract here decides whether the copy converts. Do this before any email, ad, page or sales video, and do it most thoroughly the first time you write for a product.

The output of research is a set of reusable assets:

1. A list of real day-to-day problems, fears and frustrations
2. One selected external problem, taken down through the internal and philosophical layers to the real desire
3. Claims in both emotions, made concrete
4. Named rock bottom and top of the mountain
5. A list of dualities
6. Hot keywords and a bank of angles

Related references:
- `ai-research-prompts.md` -- ready prompt templates for every step below
- `emotions-and-concreteness.md` -- steps 5-7 in full (claims, rock bottom / top of the mountain, abstraction filter)
- `core-principles.md` -- why this works (logic bubble, dualities, prosecutor mindset)

## Step 0: Perspectiving

Perspectiving is deliberately running the prospect's reality inside your own head: seeing the world through their experiences, assumptions, conclusions and beliefs rather than your own. It is like running one operating system inside another -- possible, but resource-intensive. Expect it to feel like effort and expect the urge to skip it. It is the work.

AI makes it much cheaper, because it can run the simulation with you. But keep doing part of it yourself: the act of imagining the situations is what gives you the judgment to tell a true output from a plausible-sounding one.

Everything in research answers one question: *what is their pyramid of beliefs, and what are they experiencing right now?* -- so that copy can meet them exactly there.

## Step 1: Define the Prospect and Collect Day-to-Day Problems

Start with a specific person, not a market: age, situation, what they have already tried, how they feel about where they are. Example: a 30-year-old woman who is a bit overweight, not extremely so, unhappy with what she sees in the mirror, who has tried to lose weight before but never stuck with a diet or training plan.

Then collect **real-life situations in which the problem shows itself.** The defining test for a useful situation:

> Every time it happens, it reminds them of the underlying problem and frustrates them.

Procedure (AI-assisted, see `ai-research-prompts.md` prompt 1-3):
1. Describe the prospect in detail.
2. Explain what you are trying to find and why (daily situations that act as reminders).
3. Ask for the list. Ask for real and relatable over clever.
4. Read the output critically. Mark the items that are frequent and vivid; note the ones that are rare or apply only to an extreme segment.
5. Push once more: pick a good-but-general item (e.g. "self-image") and ask for concrete moments when it happens, giving one or two examples of the kind you want (the outfit that looked great on someone online and looks wrong on her; the group photo where she is the one who stands out).
6. Finish by asking for a consolidated list of about 10 **fears and frustrations** that derive from the core problem.

What good output looks like (weight-loss example): the disappointing online clothes order, the group photo, the fitting-room mirror and lighting, eating in front of colleagues, worrying whether she is taken seriously at work, dreading the pool invitation. Many of these you would never have thought of yourself, which is the point.

Each item is already a potential hook ("Is shopping a source of stress instead of fun for you?", "Do you feel you are not taken seriously at work because of your weight?"). Note good ones as you go.

**Reading the list like a strategist:**
- *Frequency beats severity.* Health scares motivate few people because nobody gets a check-up every day. Jeans that no longer fit are noticed once and then left in the drawer. Photos and social media happen daily.
- *Segment signals.* "Getting winded playing with her kids" is a weak general lead but a very strong lead if the audience is mothers. A problem that defines a sub-audience can become a targeted angle.
- *Stage of pain.* Physical pain (joint pain) is so constant that it sells itself, but few people are at that stage. Mental pain covers far more people. Choosing which to address is a strategic decision about audience size.
- *Past failed attempts are near-universal.* Almost everyone who wants the outcome has tried and failed. "That's why every time you tried, it didn't last" implies you know their history, which builds the "they understand me" reaction.
- *Fear of missing out* connects to scarcity, one of the strongest triggers; note where it appears naturally.

## Step 2: Select ONE External Problem

The external problem is the one an outside observer would name. You will derive everything else from it, so pick one (you can repeat the process for others later, but always one at a time).

Selection criteria, in order:
1. **Happens most often** in daily life (ideally several times a week or more)
2. **Hits hardest** when it happens
3. **Acts as a reminder** of the underlying problem every single time

Why frequency comes first: the reader will re-live this moment after reading your copy, and each time they will remember who described it. A frequent trigger makes your message recur for free.

Example: "Every time she is in a photo, the first thing she sees is that she looks overweight." (Whatever someone is insecure about is the first thing they look for in a picture, and pictures are taken constantly.)

Business-opportunity example: stuck in a job they dislike with no other income to replace it, or studying something they already know they do not want to do, with no reason they can give for leaving.

## Step 3: Derive the Internal Problem

The internal problem is how the external problem makes them **feel** -- the emotional reaction, and how that reaction leaks into the rest of their day.

Ask (prompt 4): for this external problem, describe the emotional reaction in detail, break down the feelings, and show how each affects daily life. Teach the model the external/internal distinction first.

Typical families of feeling: insecurity and self-consciousness, shame and embarrassment, frustration and hopelessness, loneliness and isolation, low self-esteem, fear and anxiety.

What to look for in the answer:
- **Feelings that do not stay in the moment.** "It is not a passing feeling; it follows her into the evening and makes her watch herself instead of enjoying her friends." Spill-over is what you will use to twist the knife.
- **Self-worth statements.** "She has started measuring her worth by her weight." These are the lines that make a reader feel read.
- **Second-order effects** in other life areas (work performance, relationships, willingness to try new things). These let you connect a symptom the reader has noticed to a cause they have not -- the epiphany moment.
- **Avoidance behaviour** ("she starts dodging photos, then events"). This points directly at the philosophical problem.
- **Unrequested reframes.** The model often adds something like "this feeling is not a reflection of her real worth". That is the seed of your turn toward the solution; keep it.

Why this layer matters: speaking about the external problem is rational communication. Speaking about the internal problem is emotional communication, and a recognised feeling passes straight through the self-sealing logic bubble. This is the layer you **lead** with.

Turning it into copy -- ask, don't tell:

```
Have you ever caught yourself measuring your worth by your weight?
...
The trouble is, that view of yourself doesn't stay in the mirror.
How is work going lately? Are you performing at the level you know you can?
```

The reader answers yes, yes, then makes the connection themselves.

## Step 4: Find the Philosophical Problem (the Conflict)

The philosophical problem is the conflict the internal problem creates inside the person. It takes one of two forms:

- **A duality** -- two things they want or value pulling against each other
- **A violation of the natural order** -- something that should not be the way it is (the standard illustration: a child dying before a parent)

Find it by following the internal problem to its consequence: *because of how this makes them feel, what are they now doing, giving up, or stuck between?*

- Weight loss: the anxiety about photos grows until she avoids social gatherings altogether. Conflict: she wants a social life, and she is sacrificing it to avoid a camera.
- Business opportunity: they feel life is passing while they sit in a job or degree they do not want. Conflict: they want out, they can see that the alternative is real, and they do not know how to do it.

Usually the reader is **not aware** of this layer. Your job is to make them aware. Sequence in copy:

1. Lead with the internal problem (they already feel it -- recognition)
2. Pivot to the philosophical problem (they had not seen it -- awareness)
3. They cannot unsee it. An open conflict demands resolution: fix it or live with it.
4. If the pain was made real enough, living with it is no longer acceptable, and they start building their own story for why they should act -- and why you, who showed it to them, are the one to act with.

This is the mechanism behind "conflict creates movement". You do not close the conflict for them.

## Step 5: Name the Real Desire

Ask a prospect what they want and you get a surface answer. The stated desire is what they *believe* they want. The real desire is the resolution of the philosophical problem.

| Stated desire | Real desire |
|---|---|
| Lose 3 kg of belly fat | Look good in photos again; stop organising life around hiding; feel worth something regardless of the scale |
| Make $10K a month | Take back ownership of their own life -- stop having a boss or parents hold the keys |

How to use it:
- Keep the stated desire for call-outs and headlines -- it is how they would search for and recognise the offer.
- Speak to the real desire when you build desire and present the solution.
- Position the product as **the vehicle to the real desire** ("the vehicle that lets you take back ownership of your life"), not as the thing itself.
- The real desire is also where the top-of-the-mountain name should come from (see `emotions-and-concreteness.md`).

**One-line summary of the chain to record:**

```
External:      [observable, frequent situation]
Internal:      [how it makes them feel + how it spills into daily life]
Philosophical: [the conflict / what they are sacrificing]
Stated desire: [what they say they want]
Real desire:   [what resolving the conflict would give them]
```

## Step 6: Build Claims, Scenarios and Dualities

Covered in detail in `emotions-and-concreteness.md`. In order:

1. **Claims in two emotions** -- for each problem, one claim moving away from pain and one moving toward pleasure.
2. **Abstraction filter** -- rewrite every abstract claim as a concrete scene or number.
3. **Rock bottom and top of the mountain** -- vivid descriptions of both ends, then a memorable name for each.
4. **Duality list** -- go back through everything produced so far and list every pair of opposites (enemy vs. tool, conventional vs. unconventional, stated vs. real desire, the two named states). These get reused in every later piece.

## Step 7: Hot Keywords and Angles

Purpose: never again wonder "what do I write about next?" after the twentieth email, and give readers **insights rather than information**. An insight is a moment where two things they had not connected suddenly connect. That is what hooks people on a message.

### 7a. Hot keywords

Every niche has a small set of words that run through almost every conversation in it and carry emotional weight. They are either causes of the problem or caused by it.

- Identify 10-20 yourself first, from your problem analysis. Doing it by hand is part of perspectiving, and AI lists here tend to be mediocre unless well briefed.
- Then ask AI to extend the list (prompt 8).
- Prefer charged, concrete words over neutral category words. Weight-loss example: diet, starvation, hunger, weight, scale, food, exercise, photos, clothing, judgment, confidence, body image. ("Nutrition", "lifestyle change" are weaker.)

### 7b. Angle methods

For each keyword, create a short narrative (1-3 sentences) that approaches the word from a direction the audience has not heard before. Name the methods explicitly in the prompt -- leaving them out gives noticeably blander results.

| # | Method | What it does | Example |
|---|--------|--------------|---------|
| 1 | **News effect** | Presents something as a new discovery or invention | "New research suggests the body has a weight set point -- and it may be the missing piece." |
| 2 | **Question existing truths** | Contrarian: flips a belief the niche takes for granted | "Is the no-carb trend actually holding back your weight loss?" |
| 3 | **Show a new perspective** | A different way of looking at something familiar | "What if the hunger you feel isn't for food?" |
| 4 | **Enhance the real problem / desire** | Goes deeper into a fear, frustration or want they already have | "Still hungry after every meal? Here's why starvation diets fail." |
| 5 | **Your own theories and beliefs** | A distinctive point of view that people follow you for | A named concept only you talk about |

Methods 2 and 3 overlap; both exist to produce insight. Method 5 is the most valuable long-term, because a distinct belief system is what makes an audience follow one voice over another.

### 7c. What makes an angle strong

- **It opens a gap.** "What if the hunger isn't for food?" forces the question "then what is it for?" People need to close an open loop, which drives opens, clicks and watch time.
- **It contains a mechanism.** A named cause-and-effect explanation (hunger hormones, metabolic adaptation, weight set point) explains why the problem exists, why past attempts failed and why your solution is needed. Strong long-form promotions usually revolve around a single such mechanism; a good angle can become the centre of an entire sales video (lead with "the conventional approach is hurting you", reveal the mechanism, then present your signature solution as the way to apply it).
- **It supports your contrarian position.** Angles that give a reason why the common approach backfires reinforce the conventional-vs-unconventional duality.
- **It can be backed up.** A news or mechanism angle commits you to explaining the reasoning. Do not present invented research as fact; if there is no real basis, frame it as your perspective (method 3 or 5) instead.

Iterate: pick the 4-5 most charged keywords and ask for more narratives on just those, then re-run with the methods listed. Angles also fall out of earlier steps -- every paragraph of a rock-bottom description tends to hit a different life area (work, relationship, shopping, food) and each is an angle.

### 7d. Proven angle templates

Tested angle shapes to pour the research into. Each needs a specific problem, fear or suspicion from Steps 1-4; most work because they carry a duality underneath. (`proven-angles.md` covers each one in full; this list is the quick index.)

1. "This situation means your fear is coming true" -- aggressive, use sparingly
2. "What you suspect IS happening" -- confirms a suspicion
3. "You're right about X, and that's a problem" -- validates, then redirects
4. "Fix your problem without anyone knowing" -- best for shame-based problems
5. "Facing this? Here's what to do" (also "If you have X, do Y") -- simple and direct
6. "The logical solution only makes it worse" -- contrarian
7. "It's not your fault. Do this to fix it" -- moves the blame outside the reader
8. "Why the conventional method doesn't work, and what to do instead" -- classic contrarian

Underlying drivers: confirming suspicions, contrarian positions, news, the best-kept secret, and emotional stories.

## Step 8: Validate Against Real Voices

AI gives hypotheses. Check them against what real people say and what is already selling. This is also where you collect exact wording.

### YouTube comments (qualitative pulse)
- Search the exact topic as the prospect would phrase it ("how to lose weight as a woman in her 30s"). Skip the ads; open the top organic results and read the comments.
- Look for: situations in their own words, constraints you had not considered (works from home with a toddler, never sleeps enough), repeated complaints, questions they ask.
- **Validation test:** if commenters describe the pain point in nearly the same words you chose ("I deprive myself of food I love, then binge at the weekend"), your research is on target. If not, pivot the pain points.
- Each specific comment can become an angle: a segment ("for mothers who work from home"), an "if you struggle with X, do this" email, a question hook taken directly from a question someone asked.
- A batch of pasted comments can be given to an AI tool to summarise main pains, complaints and wanted solutions.

### Ad libraries (what is already working)
- In the Meta Ad Library, set all countries and all ad categories, then search niche keywords. Also search call-to-action phrases ("learn more", "lose weight now") to surface direct-response ads.
- Signal of a winner: the same ad seen many times, or an advertiser running a very large number of ads. Nobody keeps paying for ads that lose money.
- Open the advertiser's full list, click through to the landing pages, and record: headlines, pain points hit, claims, call to action.
- Model the angle and structure; adapt to your product. Something already proven has far better odds than something never tested.

### Affiliate marketplaces (proven long-form)
- On a marketplace such as ClickBank, open the affiliate marketplace, choose the category and sort by the popularity metric (Gravity), high to low.
- Open the top offers' sales pages or video sales letters. Record the claims and especially the **mechanism** each one uses to explain the problem.
- Model the style and the type of claim; never copy text. Inventive wording matters in the headline; in body copy the claim matters more than the phrasing.

### Customer surveys (when an audience exists)
The easiest and most accurate source. Ask:
- What is your biggest frustration with [topic]?
- What is the main obstacle stopping you from [desired result]?
- What is your current situation (work, income, stage)?
- What is your goal?
- What do you like / dislike about [product or program]?

How to read the answers:
- **Repetition is the signal.** If three people in a row say the same thing ("nobody around me has any ambition; I'm the only one thinking this way"), that is a headline-level pain.
- Stated goals will cluster on the surface desire (everyone writes the same number). The frustrations and stories around it reveal the real desire.
- When people say why they like a feature, they are telling you which problem it solves. Mirror it: state the problem as a question, then present the feature as the answer ("Feel like you're walking this path alone? That's why we built the community calls.").
- Lift their phrases. Good copy here is mostly reading what the audience says and saying it back.

## Rules of Thumb

- Most of the available time goes to research; writing is the short part.
- One external problem at a time, taken all the way down.
- Frequent beats dramatic.
- Ask questions the reader will answer "yes" to; let them draw the conclusion.
- You are not trying to be creative. You are trying to be accurate about someone's life.
- Note every angle the moment it appears.
- If real voices contradict the AI output, the real voices win.
