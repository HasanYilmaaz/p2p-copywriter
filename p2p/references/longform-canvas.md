# P2P Long-Form / Launch Canvas

The planning document you fill in BEFORE writing any long-form sales copy (VSL, sales letter, multi-episode launch event). The canvas is the backbone; the script is only the canvas expanded.

Companion files:
- `offer-construction.md` -- canvas step 4 in depth (pillars, pitch order, price, urgency, guarantee)
- `leads-and-packaging.md` -- canvas step 6 in depth (awareness levels, lead types, lead checklist) plus packaging a sales video for YouTube
- `vsl-breakdown.md` -- annotated breakdown of a complete VSL

---

## 1. What Counts as Long-Form

Long-form is defined by the JOB, not the word count. It is the main conversion asset of a business: the piece most ads, emails and sales conversations send traffic to, and where most revenue is closed. It can be a 5-minute video, a 45-minute VSL, a written sales letter, or a 3-4 episode event.

- Use the long-form method whenever the goal of the piece is to SELL a product. Selling needs far more convincing than getting a click or a registration.
- The medium does not decide it. An email that only drives a click to the sales video is short-form; an email that has to make the sale itself is long-form. A normal YouTube content video is short-form; a YouTube video that ends in an offer is long-form.
- The planning work is identical for 15 minutes or 4 hours. Shorter is often harder, because every argument has to land fast.
- Length rule: copy is never too long, only too boring. It should be exactly as long as it takes to walk through every link in the chain of beliefs -- and not one argument longer.
- Adapt to the medium. Classic direct-mail headlines and structures do not transfer unchanged to YouTube or social platforms. The same argument must be packaged for the platform it lives on (see `leads-and-packaging.md`).

**What the whole thing is for:** lead the prospect to ONE inevitable conclusion (the marketing thesis). When they accept it, buying is simply the next logical step. Along the way you are building *visceral desire*: you name their real problem and real desire so precisely that they start telling themselves the story of why they need the solution.

## 2. The Canvas at a Glance (Fill in This Order)

| Step | Element | Question it answers |
|------|---------|---------------------|
| 0 | Problem & Desire | What is the ONE problem and ONE desire this promotion is built on? |
| 1 | Duality & Common Enemy | Who do they become if they win / lose, and who is in the way? |
| 2 | Marketing Thesis (One Belief) | What single thing must they believe for the sale to be the next step? |
| 3 | Big Idea (+ Unique Proposition) | What is the theme/angle that packages the whole promotion? |
| 4 | Offer (Pillars) | What gaps must the narrative open so the offer fills them? |
| 5 | Chain of Beliefs (Marketing Argument) | What must they believe, in what order, and what proves each? |
| 6 | Lead | How do we open so we meet them where they are? |

Why this order: each step is derived from the one before. The thesis comes out of the problem and the duality; the pillars come out of the thesis; the chain of beliefs is reverse-engineered from the thesis AND the pillars; the lead is chosen last because it is simply the first link of the chain, matched to the audience's awareness. The Big Idea is slot 3, but it often only shows up while you are working on later steps -- leave a placeholder and come back.

Also decide early how the piece will be packaged for its platform (title/topic), because that constrains the theme you write under.

## 3. Step 0 -- Problem & Desire (Principle of the One Thing)

Build the promotion around one overarching problem and one desire. You may touch other problems, but each must trace back to the one big problem that causes them. Several problems mean several solutions, and then no single conclusion is inevitable. This holds even when the product does many things: pick the one problem the whole bundle answers.

Fill five layers:

| Layer | What it is | Weight-loss example |
|-------|-----------|---------------------|
| Desire (surface) | What they say they want | Lose 3 kg of belly fat |
| External problem | What is visibly happening | They look heavy in photos |
| Internal problem | How that makes them feel | They no longer enjoy being photographed and avoid it |
| Philosophical problem | The conflict/duality you pivot on | They start avoiding going out at all, which makes the anxiety worse |
| Real desire | What they actually want underneath | To look good in pictures again |

Rules for filling it:

- **The surface desire can open the message, but you must pivot.** It is shallow; the selling power is in the philosophical problem and the real desire.
- **Choose the internal and philosophical problem that lead to YOUR solution.** The same audience and the same surface desire ("make $10K/month online") were framed two ways in two launches: (a) "I know online business can free me, I just don't know which one" for a single business-model course, and (b) "I chose the conventional path but expect unconventional results" for a bundle of several skill paths. Different product, different conflict. Decide here which conflict your product resolves.
- **Read what the audience has already decided.** In example (a) the audience had already chosen "online business" as the vehicle and was stuck on which one. So the theme became "the best online business model for a beginner", not "how to make $10K a month". The philosophical problem tells you how to theme and title the piece.
- **Validate against real audience language** (surveys, comments, calls). If their own words do not match your layers, the layers are wrong.
- **The narrative for the solution starts here.** Roughly three quarters of the piece is not the pitch. If the solution is only on your mind when you reach the pitch, the transition will feel abrupt. Pick the conflict now so that every minute builds the need for what you sell.

## 4. Step 1 -- Duality & Common Enemy

Name three things:

- **Top of the Mountain** -- the identity they become when they succeed
- **Rock Bottom** -- the identity they are (or will be) if they do not act
- **Common Enemy** -- what "we" throw rocks at; it creates us-vs-them and lets you tell them it was not their fault

| Promotion | Top of Mountain | Rock Bottom | Common Enemy |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|--------------|
| Self-development/business event | New Renaissance Man | Modern-Day Slave | The Puppet Masters (unseen elites pulling strings) |
| Multi-path education launch | Owner of your own future | Owned by others | The conventional path |
| Weight loss | Picture-Perfect Fit | Prisoner of the Plate | Traditional depriving diets |

**Generating questions.** Answer in concrete, real-life detail so you can paint before/after repeatedly:

Top of the Mountain
1. What is the consequence of having the solution? What do they actually achieve?
2. What positive feelings does that create?
3. What is one action that represents the desire fulfilled? (e.g. doing what he wants, when he wants, with whom he wants)

Rock Bottom
1. What is the consequence of the problem? (e.g. "someone else now owns you")
2. What negative feelings does that create?
3. What must the prospect accept, realise and decide to get out?

Common Enemy
1. Who or what are they? 2. What consequence do they bring on the prospect?

**Why this matters:** a strong duality provokes. Provocation creates discomfort, and discomfort is what produces action. The angrier people are at their problem, the higher the conversion. The instructor's own review of past launches found the duality was the single most important element, and that a launch which under-used it left results on the table.

**First draft of the belief chain falls out of Rock Bottom question 3.** Example: (1) accept that you are enslaved, (2) realise every regular career is a different road to the same place, (3) decide to take an alternative road. Then ask "how do I prove each one?" -- that is already an outline.

**Calibrate the negative emotion.** A launch built mainly on fear and moving away from pain can convert extremely well and still leave a sour sentiment around the brand. Use Rock Bottom to provoke, but let the dominant note be the pull toward the Top of the Mountain. Inspire rather than frighten.

## 5. Step 2 -- Marketing Thesis (The One Belief)

Think like a prosecutor: before arguing anything, fix the exact thesis the jury must accept. In copy that thesis is the one belief which, once held, makes the sale the natural next step. Use the One Belief formula (credited to Russell Brunson):

> **[NEW OPPORTUNITY]** is the key to **[THEIR DESIRE]**, and it is only attainable through **[MY NEW MECHANISM]**.

| Case | New opportunity | Desire | New mechanism |
|------|-----------------|--------|---------------|
| P90X | Avoiding the plateau effect | Building muscle | Muscle confusion system |
| Invisalign | Aligning teeth without ugly braces | The smile you want without disrupting your life | Invisalign's proprietary technology |
| Subway | Eating healthy fast food | Losing weight | A diet based on Subway sandwiches |
| Local service | The easiest, most profitable way for a pub to win customers online | New customers | Location-based YouTube ads |
| Evergreen business VSL | Starting with the most beginner-friendly business model | Zero to $10K/month in record time | Selling ROI services |
| Education launch | Renouncing the conventional path | Unconventional results | Becoming an owner of your own future |
| Event launch | Achieving financial, location and time freedom | Working your way into the top 1% | Becoming a New Renaissance Man |

How to write a strong thesis:

- **The mechanism must feel new and be yours.** A mechanism the market has not heard before removes you from comparison: nobody else sells it.
- **Best version: make the Top of the Mountain the mechanism.** "...only attainable through becoming [Top of Mountain identity]." People do not only want to *have* a result, they want to *be* that person. The offer pillars then define what that identity consists of (step 4). The instructor's highest-converting launches used this form.
- **Build the duality into the thesis** where possible (conventional path vs. unconventional results).
- **Abstract terms are allowed here, on one condition:** the promotion must spend its length giving them meaning (see Anchoring below).
- **The opportunity doubles as the curiosity hook.** "The most beginner-friendly business model" makes people want to find out which one it is.
- **Name the mechanism by volume.** Brainstorm many candidate names and discard most; the first name is rarely the one that sticks (see `vsl-breakdown.md`).

**Anchoring.** Coined terms such as "conventional path", "modern-day slave", "access" mean nothing on first read. Throughout the piece you load them with the audience's own problems and desires. Once loaded, the single word recalls the whole emotion: "this is happening because you are still on the conventional path". Practical consequences:
- Introduce each coined term early, define it through concrete scenes, and repeat it.
- Use the Rock Bottom term to reopen the pain later in one line instead of re-explaining it.
- Keep the number of coined terms small (the duality pair, the enemy, the mechanism), or none of them will carry weight.
- Terms you give meaning to become yours; competitors who copy them remind the market of you.

## 6. Step 3 -- Big Idea & Unique Proposition

The Big Idea is the theme/angle the promotion is wrapped in -- the one compelling central idea that ties the thesis, the chain and the offer together. The lack of one is described (after Todd Brown) as the single biggest cause of poor conversion. The same product can be sold with or without one: "How I started and scaled my marketing agency" (no idea) versus "The Digital Renaissance is here" (big idea) were promotions for essentially the same offer.

**Recognising one**

| Line | Verdict | Why |
|------|---------|-----|
| "5 growing REITs with high dividend payouts" | No big idea | A normal promise seen hundreds of times a day |
| "The secret to Wall Street's fastest profits... going public for the first time" | Good claim, not a big idea | Nothing connects two things in a new way |
| "Outlawed for 41 years, now legal again... this gold investment launched the largest family fortune ever" | Big idea | A secret, a "new" opening, a promise and a mechanism |
| "There's a new railroad across America, and it's making some people very rich" | Big idea | Joins a familiar wealth story to something unknown; reader must find out what it is |
| "The Digital Renaissance" | Big idea | Two known concepts fused; implies a historical turning point happening now |

The pattern to aim for: **the best-kept secret hidden in plain sight** -- something the reader feels they should already know about but somehow missed. The engine is the same as a good joke: two familiar things connected in a way nobody expected.

**Checklist (attributed to Michael Masterson).** Run every candidate through it:
1. Emotionally compelling -- it triggers intrigue, greed or fear, not just interest
2. Contains the primary promise (stated or clearly implied)
3. Carries or sets up the unique mechanism (helpful, not mandatory)
4. Intellectually interesting -- "what is that? I need to know more"

**Supporting tests (attributed to Todd Brown).** Not all are required, but each one you can honestly tick makes it stronger:
- ONE idea. Two 5/10 ideas combined make a 3/10, because attention is split.
- A big but believable promise. The bigger the claim, the bigger the proof it will need; too-good-to-be-true raises guards.
- Unique -- an angle they have not seen.
- Sounds new: a new opportunity, discovery, loophole, piece of research.
- Urgent for an external reason: a macro change happening in the world whether or not they act. This is different from cart deadlines. A secret is urgent by nature, because people want it before everyone else has it.
- Specific and concrete.
- Easy to grasp. If they have to think to decode it, it fails.
- Has a track record (a past result, or a historical precedent that "happened before").
- Easy to apply through a mechanism ("...for beginners").

**How to find one.** You cannot produce it by staring at the page. It comes from heavy research and from writing the other canvas steps, followed by time away (walk, gym) while the connections form. Method for a model: collect candidate analogies from history, other industries and current events that mirror the audience's situation; draft 8-10 candidates; score each against the checklist; keep one. If the Big Idea involves a historical or factual parallel, the facts must be real -- research them or ask the user; do not invent.

**Unique Proposition.** The Big Idea is usually two or three words and never stands alone. Write a 2-4 sentence expansion that unpacks it and flows straight into the lead:

```
[What is happening / what some people are doing] +
[why it matters to you, the stakes] +
[the only way through = mechanism / top of mountain] +
[what I will show you]
```

- Event example: a new era is approaching; a list of visible symptoms; "there's a bigger story that isn't being told"; the only protection is to become [identity]; on [date] I'll show you how.
- Evergreen example: ordinary 20-year-olds are using this beginner-friendly model to get paid monthly by small businesses without doing the delivery themselves; it is the same strategy large marketplace companies use; experts have a name for it.

Headlines written on the canvas (Big Idea line, Unique Proposition) are for the lead and the sales page. A YouTube title is a different thing, written separately: short and broad (see `leads-and-packaging.md`, Part B).

## 7. Step 4 -- Offer Pillars (Summary)

Map the offer BEFORE the chain of beliefs, because the chain has to open the gaps the offer will fill. Thinking in deliverables or even in benefits is too flat; think in **pillars**: the few things a person needs in order to become the Top of the Mountain. Each pillar maps to part of the offer, and each needs its own narrative seeded from the first minute.

Example: "What makes a New Renaissance Man?" -> knowledge + a business (the course), community (student group), accountability (coaches, Q&A, peers), self-improvement (bonus course).

The usual mistake is a two-act piece: content for 75-80% and then a sudden turn into selling. The audience feels ambushed. When the pillars have been seeded all along, the reaction is "that is exactly what I need".

Seeding is concrete, not only thematic: a programme, a student result or a company that the audience first hears about in the pitch is too steep a turn. Each one must be named earlier in the body.

Full method, pitch order, price presentation, urgency and guarantee: `offer-construction.md`.

## 8. Step 5 -- Chain of Beliefs (The Marketing Argument)

"We are not selling, we are solving." You are assembling a puzzle: the set of beliefs, in order, that ends in the thesis.

### 8.1 Build it backwards

1. End point: they buy the offer.
2. Just before: they believe the pillars are what they need (the offer supplies them).
3. Before that: they believe the marketing thesis.
4. Keep asking "what must they already believe to accept this?" and step back one belief at a time...
5. ...until you reach something they already believe today. That is where the lead starts.

Then read it forwards as a paragraph. If it reads as one smooth argument, you have the whole promotion in miniature.

Worked example (event launch), forwards:
1. There is a hidden war going on behind the scenes. *(meets them inside what they already suspect)*
2. It is about control: the enemy wants you weak, because weak people obey.
3. This has happened before -- a dark age.
4. After it came one of the most prosperous periods in history -- a renaissance.
5. History repeats: a new renaissance is coming, but those who do not act get stuck in the new dark age.
6. The only way out is to become [Top of Mountain].
7. Becoming that takes five things: [pillars].
8. The best way to get all five is [offer].

Worked example (final episode of an education launch):
1. "Information is the new gold" is outdated; information is now abundant, so it is cheap.
2. Resources are not the secret either.
3. Success comes down to ACCESS: to the right opportunities, the right knowledge, the right people.
4. Why each kind of access matters and why you lack it today (algorithms built for consumption on one side, broad outdated curricula on the other).
5. So I am giving people access -> offer (three pillars = three kinds of access).

In a multi-episode event the chain is split across episodes; earlier episodes plant beliefs the last one cashes in. Episode 1 of that launch established "the internet is the place to be", "the only difference between me and you is that I refused the conventional path", and "play to your strengths, not your weaknesses" -- which set up episode 2's presentation of several paths.

### 8.2 Questions that surface the beliefs

1. What must they believe about **you**?
2. What must they believe about **themselves**? This is the one most often missed. Many people do not buy because they doubt that *they* can do it, not because they doubt you or the product.
3. What must they believe about the **marketing thesis**?
4. What must they believe about **other solutions**? Essential in sophisticated, crowded markets where buyers are shopping around: acknowledge the alternatives and show why yours is the best fit. The instructor's technique for this is **parametrization**: you set the comparison criteria, score the alternatives honestly in a visible table, and your solution must genuinely come out best. Rules and example in `vsl-breakdown.md` (section 2.5).

### 8.3 Rules

- **Claim + proof = belief.** A claim without a proof point is only a claim. Every link gets at least one proof.
- **It is a chain.** Skip one link and everything after it fails. Order matters.
- **Straight line (after Jordan Belfort).** Every argument must move the prospect toward the thesis. An argument that is interesting but goes "upwards" rather than forwards gets cut; it distracts and costs attention. Check each section: does this move them closer to accepting the thesis?
- **Let them reach the conclusion themselves.** Use short agreement questions ("If you had access to better opportunities, would you be better off? ...and if you knew how to use them?").
- **Start inside their world.** Opening links should confirm suspicions they already hold. This gives the message internal coherence and builds rapport before you ask them to believe anything new.
- **Each link can be written with the short-form structures** (verify -> internal problem -> philosophical pivot -> explanation -> benefit). Long-form is those structures repeated once per belief.

### 8.4 Proof-point toolbox

| Proof type | Use | Example |
|-----------|-----|---------|
| Story analogy | Make an abstract claim feel obvious | Knowledge as water: once a single oasis, now a flood people drown in |
| Simple analogy | One-line logic transfer | An unlimited gold mine would make gold worthless -- same with information |
| Real-world examples | Disprove a common assumption | The biggest ride and lodging companies own no cars and no rooms |
| Pure logic | When the statement proves itself | You cannot follow the conventional path and expect unconventional results |
| Historical precedent | "It happened before, so it can again" | Dark age followed by renaissance |
| Science / named law | Borrow authority | An exponential-growth law for "online moves faster" |
| Before vs. after | Let them see the change | What the internet looked like two years ago |
| Cautionary examples | Show the cost of inaction | Professions already displaced by new technology |
| Authority | Belief about you | Results you have produced in this exact field |
| Personal experience | Relatability plus proof | "On the traditional path I would be where most people are" |
| Success stories / track record | Belief about themselves | Ordinary students who did it |
| The prospect's own situation | Self-generated proof | "Of today's decisions, how many were really yours?" |
| Damaging admission | Raises credibility of everything after | "For years I told you there was one way. I was wrong." |
| Objection reversal | Turn the objection into an advantage | "You don't need to be a good writer; good writers struggle with this" |
| "Made for you" fit device | Belief about themselves + not your fault | A personality-type match showing which path suits them; past failures become "wrong vehicle for you" |

Template for presenting an opportunity/path inside the chain: remove the main pain point -> benefit -> gain potential (appeal to greed) -> ease (appeal to laziness) -> risk reversal -> track record/testimonials -> objection reversal -> science or fit device.

Drafting analogies with AI: specify (a) what the analogy is about, (b) the argument it must make, (c) the conclusion it must land on. Never fabricate proof: testimonials, numbers and studies must come from the user or from verified research. Analogies and logic are the proof types you may create freely.

## 9. Step 6 -- Lead (Summary)

The lead is where the chain meets the prospect: its first link, delivered in a form that matches how aware they are. More aware -> more direct (offer, invitation, promise). Less aware -> more indirect (problem-solution, secret/system, proclamation, story). A lead that works for a less-aware audience also works for a more-aware one, not the reverse. When in doubt, use problem-solution.

A lead must: expand on the Big Idea, sell the viewership, set the stakes (a reason to watch now), and give proof of the promise when the claim touches money or health.

Full guidance: `leads-and-packaging.md`.

## 11. Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---------|--------------|-----|
| Starting from a blank page | No backbone; arguments wander | Fill the canvas first |
| Several problems or ideas | Attention split; no single conclusion | One problem, one desire, one Big Idea |
| Thinking about the offer only at the pitch | Abrupt turn; audience feels ambushed | Map pillars at step 4, seed from minute one |
| Thesis without a new mechanism | You are comparable to everyone | Name a mechanism; ideally the Top of Mountain identity |
| Coined terms never given meaning | Abstract and empty | Anchor them through concrete scenes and repetition |
| Claims without proof | No belief is formed | One proof per link, minimum |
| Missing link in the chain | Everything after it collapses | Read the chain forwards; ask "would they already accept this?" |
| Interesting but irrelevant sections | Leaves the straight line; bores | Cut anything that does not advance the thesis |
| Ignoring "can I do it?" | The most common hidden objection | Include beliefs about themselves, with relatable proof |
| Under-using the duality | The main source of movement is lost | Return to Top of Mountain vs. Rock Bottom throughout, and at the close |
| Overdoing fear | Converts, but poisons sentiment | Provoke, then inspire |
| Drifting from fundamentals when experienced | "Freestyling" drops key elements | Re-run the canvas every time |
