# The 2 Copy Emotions, Rock Bottom vs. Top of the Mountain, and the Abstraction Filter

This file turns raw research (problems, fears, desires -- see `research-framework.md`) into usable copy material: claims, two vivid scenarios, two names, and a concreteness pass that removes the #1 conversion killer. Prompt templates for each exercise are in `ai-research-prompts.md`.

Order of work:
1. Write claims in both emotions
2. Describe rock bottom and top of the mountain
3. Name the two states
4. Run everything through the abstraction filter

---

## 1. The 2 Copy Emotions

Every claim or promise can point in one of two directions:

| Moving toward pleasure (goal, desired outcome) | Moving away from pain (fear, frustration, problem) |
|---|---|
| "Become confident in social situations again" | "Stop feeling anxious in social situations" |
| "Take pictures and be proud of how you look" | "Stop looking overweight in pictures" |
| "Command respect from your co-workers" | "People will stop not taking you seriously at work" |
| "Finally be at peace with yourself" | "Stop judging your worth by your weight" |

### Why away-from-pain is stronger

People respond roughly **twice as strongly** to avoiding a loss as to gaining the same thing. The brain optimises for survival, not for thriving: getting by is safe, reaching for more costs effort and risk.

Illustration: offer someone $100 if they walk three kilometres, and most will not bother. Tell them they will be charged $100 unless they walk three kilometres to pay a parking ticket, and most will go. The amount is identical. The gain feels **optional** ("I can skip it"). The loss feels like **no choice** ("I do this or I lose").

### How to use both

- Produce both versions of every claim. Most copy uses them interchangeably, but each use should be **deliberate**: decide which emotion a sentence needs before writing it.
- The away-from-pain version is usually easier to write first, because research produces problems: state the problem and say it stops. Then flip it into the desire.
- Aim for at least 4-10 claim pairs per project. More raw material makes every later piece faster.
- Derive claims from specific findings, not generic benefits. Each pair above comes from one researched problem: the philosophical problem (avoiding social events), the selected external problem (photos), a fear (not taken seriously at work) and an internal problem (worth tied to weight).
- **Placement matters.** A claim that answers the philosophical problem ("confident in social situations again") lands best *after* you have led with the internal problem and made the reader aware of that conflict. Before that they do not yet know it is their problem. Not every claim needs this setup, but the deep ones do.
- When a tool or an edit rewrites a claim, check that it did not silently switch emotion. Concrete rewrites tend to drift toward pleasure. If you wanted pain, turn it back.

### Guardrail: do not build a whole promotion on fear

Negative emotion works very well, and overusing it has a cost. Used too aggressively it produces sales now and backlash later, especially from existing customers who see how new ones are being sold and feel their fears are being preyed on. Fear-led messaging also polarises (politics is the standard example) -- useful to politicians, damaging to a business long-term.

Operating rule:
- Lead with pain; use touches of it throughout.
- Do not make negative emotion the main emotion of an entire campaign.
- Always close on the positive: "but it doesn't have to be that way -- there is a way out, and it looks like this."
- Niches that are inherently fear-driven (survival/preparedness, for example) tolerate more. In most niches, the default tone of the promotion is positive.

## 2. Rock Bottom vs. Top of the Mountain

Two scenarios that mark the two ends of the journey. They are the main duality of most promotions and you will play between them at every length, from a short email to a 10,000-word script.

| | Rock bottom | Top of the mountain |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | The worst situation the prospect can end up in | The state reached by someone who completed the journey |
| Built from | Their fears and frustrations, all come true | Their desired outcomes, wants and aspirations, all come true |
| Framing | "You are already heading this way. Keep the same habits and this is where it leads." | "This is the physical and mental state you look up to today." |
| Emotion | Away from pain | Toward pleasure |

### How to build them

Ask for (or write) both scenarios described **with as much tangible, concrete detail as possible**, covering both physical and mental state. Explain each concept before asking (prompt 6).

Each scenario should walk through the life areas found in research, one per paragraph: body and mirror, clothes, social life and photos, relationships, work, the relationship with the core object of the niche (food, money, time). A scenario that touches every area gives you one angle per paragraph.

### What to mine from the output

- **Flip pairs.** A line like "she sees food as a tool for nourishment rather than an enemy" is a ready-made duality (enemy vs. tool) and can carry an entire ad or sales video ("how to turn food from your enemy into your tool").
- **Inner contradictions.** "She fears food and yet is obsessed with it." Describing a tension the reader lives with but has never put into words is the fastest way to win them over.
- **Chains from one life area to another.** Example: relationship trouble caused by weight, *but not the way they think* -- not because the partner minds, but because she dislikes how she looks, avoids photos, then avoids going out, and the shrinking social life strains the relationship. "X is causing Y, but not the way you think" is a strong angle form.
- **Question openers.** Convert a vivid line into a question the reader answers yes to: "Do you feel trapped in a body you don't recognise?"
- **Closing lines.** The top-of-the-mountain description usually contains an uplifting sentence that works as the positive ending of a piece.

Notice the difference in force: the rock-bottom text almost always hits harder than the top-of-the-mountain text. That is the 2x effect in practice -- and the reason for the guardrail above.

### How to use them in copy

Put people into two buckets and let them choose:

```
One person treats [thing] as an enemy. The other uses it as a tool.
Their results are exact opposites.
Which one are you?
If you want to stay here, that's your choice.
If you want to be there, I have something for you.
```

You do not argue them across. You describe both sides so clearly that staying on the wrong side becomes uncomfortable, and you are the one who showed the route to the other side. Do not resolve the conflict for them (see Theory of Dualities in `core-principles.md`).

## 3. Naming the Two States

Do not call them "rock bottom" and "top of the mountain" in the copy. Give each a name the audience can be told "you can become this, or you can become that" -- and repeat the pair throughout the promotion.

Examples:
- Business opportunity: "master of your own future" vs. "slave of your fears"
- Men's self-development: "top 1% men" vs. "modern-day slave"
- Weight loss: "picture-perfect fit" vs. "prisoner of the plate"

### Criteria for a good name

1. **Self-explanatory.** Hearing it once is enough to understand the whole narrative. "Prisoner of the plate" needs no explanation.
2. **Tangible and concrete.** The reader can see themselves in that situation.
3. **Tied to daily life.** It refers to something they actually experience.
4. **Aspirational** (top) or **something they want to escape at all costs** (bottom).
5. **Memorable.** People should be able to repeat it.

### What to reject

Alliterative but empty labels such as "victorious vanguard", "radiant revivalist", "mindful maven", "confidence conqueror", "dazzling dynamo". They sound like names but have no clear meaning in practice, so nobody remembers them or knows what they promise. "Abstract" is the usual failure.

### Process

- Give the model the concept plus two or three example pairs from other niches, then ask for names.
- Ask for **ten options at a time** rather than one; quality is uneven.
- When rejecting, say why, and say what you liked about the one that worked ("I can see myself in 'prisoner of the plate'; the other has no clear meaning in practice -- relate it more to her daily life").
- Expect to finish it yourself. The best top-of-the-mountain name usually comes from the **real desire** found in research. If the core pain is how she looks in photos and the real desire is to look good in them again, combine a decent candidate ("fabulously fit") with that desire to get "picture-perfect fit".
- Check the pair together: "Are you going to be a [bottom] or a [top]?" should read as an obvious choice.

The same name can extend to the solution ("the method that makes you picture-perfect fit"), tying the signature solution to the real desire.

## 4. The #1 Conversion Killer: Abstraction

Abstraction is a communication bias most writers do not notice in their own work. Removing it makes copy markedly stronger.

### Why it kills response

- People react to a tangible stimulus far more than to an abstract one. A stranger saying "you are in danger" gets a puzzled look. The same words from someone holding a weapon get an immediate reaction. Same sentence, different concreteness.
- Abstract words mean different things to different readers. Ask 100 people what "the love of your life" or "financial freedom" looks like and you get 100 answers. Ask what "$10,436.26 landing in your bank account every month" means and you get one.
- Copy must leave **no room for interpretation**. Every reader should arrive at exactly the conclusion you intended. An abstract claim hands the conclusion over to the reader's own pyramid of beliefs (the blind men and the elephant again).

### Why we do it without noticing

Abstraction is how the mind carries things. One apple is an apple; many go in a basket; baskets go on a pallet; pallets on a truck. In language: "apple" becomes "red fruit" becomes "fruit". The writer says "fruit" while picturing an apple and assumes the reader pictures an apple too. The reader pictures a watermelon. Niche words such as "weight loss", "confidence", "success", "freedom", "health", "respect" are baskets.

### The rule

Communicate in the space that is **concrete, measurable, tangible and experiential**. Switch off the abstraction mechanism and name the apple.

| Abstract | Concrete |
|---|---|
| "currency" | "$5,000 in cash" |
| "signs of aging" | "wrinkles around your eyes" |
| "achieve financial freedom" | "$10,436.26 deposited into your bank account every month" |
| "be confident in social situations again" | "feel at ease at your friend's backyard barbecue this weekend" |
| "take pictures and be proud of how you look" | "stand front and centre in the family holiday photo and love the person smiling back at you" |
| "command respect from your co-workers" | "lead the Monday morning meeting knowing your colleagues are focused on your ideas, not your size" |
| "people will take you seriously at work" | "your boss trusts you to lead that important client project" |
| "stop judging your worth by your weight" | "stand tall in front of the mirror, celebrating what you've achieved, not the number on the scale" |

Why the concrete versions are stronger: each one implies you know the exact thought in the reader's head (in the meeting: "are they listening to me or looking at my size?") and promises its end in a scene they can picture.

### Running the filter

Apply it every time, after writing claims or after finishing a draft -- the order does not matter, skipping it does.

1. Scan for basket words: any noun or phrase that 100 readers would picture 100 ways.
2. Replace each with a specific scene from the prospect's daily life, a number, or a physical detail. Reuse the scenes found in research (photos, the meeting, the fitting room) so the copy stays coherent.
3. Review each replacement as the person who knows the audience:
   - **Is it true to what they want?** "Be the life of the party" overshoots someone who only wants to feel at ease. Cut it back.
   - **Is the scene frequent?** A best friend's wedding is real but rare; a party or dinner happens several times a month. Prefer the frequent scene.
   - **Did the meaning survive?** "Feel better about yourself" was meant as self-acceptance; a rewrite about morning energy has changed the claim. Restate the intent and redo it.
   - **Did the emotion flip?** If a pain claim came back as a pleasure claim, decide on purpose which you need.
4. If AI did the rewrite (prompt 5), do it in the same thread as the research so it draws on the established scenes, and correct it with reasons when it misses.

The filter can also suggest new material: a concrete line about standing front and centre in the photo surfaces the insight that this person currently hides at the back of group photos -- a detail the reader will recognise instantly.

Test for done: hand the sentence to 100 people in the audience. Do they all see the same picture?

## Output Checklist

- [ ] 4-10 claim pairs, each tied to a researched problem, both emotions
- [ ] Every claim passed through the abstraction filter and sanity-checked
- [ ] Rock-bottom scenario, one life area per paragraph, vivid
- [ ] Top-of-the-mountain scenario, same areas, vivid
- [ ] A named pair that is self-explanatory, tangible and tied to the real desire
- [ ] Flip pairs and inner contradictions extracted into the duality list
- [ ] Angles noted from each paragraph
- [ ] Negative emotion used as lead and accent, not as the whole tone; positive ending drafted
