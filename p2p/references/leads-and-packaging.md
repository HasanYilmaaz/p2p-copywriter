# P2P Leads & Sales-Video Packaging

Two decisions that determine whether anyone watches the argument you built:
- **Part A -- The Lead** (Launch Canvas step 6): how the piece opens, matched to audience awareness.
- **Part B -- Packaging & distribution**: choosing the medium and packaging a sales video (topic, title, thumbnail, retention) so the platform keeps showing it.

Read with `longform-canvas.md`. For a line-by-line example of a finished lead, see `vsl-breakdown.md`.

# Part A -- The Lead

The lead is the opening of the promotion. Its job is to meet the prospect where they are: start with something they have already seen, felt or suspected, so the message passes through their existing beliefs and feels coherent. It is chosen LAST, after the chain of beliefs exists, because it is the first link of that chain put in the right form.

## A1. Five Levels of Awareness (Eugene Schwartz)

| Level | The prospect... | What the opening has to do |
|-------|-----------------|----------------------------|
| Unaware | does not know they have a problem, let alone a solution | Create awareness of the problem |
| Problem aware | knows the problem, has never considered that a solution exists; is living with it | Make the pain felt so strongly that they must look for a way out |
| Solution aware | knows solutions exist, has not decided which is best | Show which solution, and why |
| Product aware | knows the kinds of solutions on the market, but not your specific product | Introduce your product/mechanism |
| Most aware | knows you, your product and your competitors | Give the deal |

Example (back pain): solution aware = "exercise, a better chair or medication could fix this"; product aware = knows those categories well but not your specific method; most aware = knows exactly who offers what.

**Sizing rule.** Each step down in awareness is a larger pool of people. Direct leads convert a small, hot audience; indirect leads take longer to reach the product but can reach far more people. Moving to a more indirect lead is how you expand a market once the direct claims have reached everyone they can.

Related concept, **market sophistication**: in a new market a plain promise works. As competitors repeat the same promise it stops working, and you have to add a mechanism to the claim. When everyone has a mechanism, you need a different mechanism or system altogether. A crowded market pushes you toward mechanism-led and secret-led openings.

## A2. Lead Types, from Most Direct to Most Indirect

The classification follows Michael Masterson's *Great Leads*.

| # | Lead type | Opens with | Best for |
|---|-----------|-----------|----------|
| 1 | Offer | Product, price, discount, guarantee -- in the headline or first lines | Most aware; low-commitment or familiar products |
| 2 | Invitation | A softer, exclusive-sounding offer | Most aware; higher-priced offers that need some convincing |
| 3 | Promise | A claim about the result, not the product | Solution aware, not yet product aware |
| 4 | Problem-Solution | The problem, then a pivot to the solution | Problem aware and above. The default |
| 5 | Secret / System | A hidden method or little-known system | Solution-aware buyers in crowded markets |
| 6 | Proclamation | A bold prediction or declaration | Unaware; expanding the audience |
| 7 | Story | A narrative | Unaware and sceptical markets |

### 1. Offer lead
Lead with the deal itself. Works when the audience already knows the product and the seller and only needs the terms (a known author's book for $1; a familiar consumer product at a price). In that situation an indirect opening would *lower* conversion: they do not need a story, they need the price.

### 2. Invitation lead
Still an offer, but framed as a selective invitation. Use it when the audience is aware but the price point is too high to open with. Pattern: "Would you like me to personally [big result] for you, [on unusually favourable terms]?" The framing implies it is not for everyone, which adds desire.

### 3. Promise lead
Open with the result they will experience. The big promise is the headline, the first line and often the last line. Examples of the form: "Reduce your wrinkles by up to 56% in the first month"; classic how-to book titles are promise leads. Fits prospects who know a solution is possible but need to be brought to yours.
Caution: bare promises wear out as a market matures. Add a mechanism to the claim ("How [named method] helps [result], using nothing more than [surprisingly simple thing]").

### 4. Problem-Solution lead
Open with the problem, as a question or a statement ("Can't fall asleep? Waking too early?"; "Why does your English let you down?"), verify it, move to the internal and philosophical problem, then to the solution. This is the same structure used throughout P2P short-form copy.
- Works for problem-aware prospects and every level above.
- Does NOT work for people unaware of the problem.
- The recommended starting point for anyone not yet experienced with indirect leads, and the safe choice when awareness is uncertain.

### 5. Secret / System lead
People are drawn to secrets and want to close the information gap, ideally before everyone else knows. Frame the solution as **the best-kept secret hidden in plain sight**: something they should know about, yet somehow nobody does.
- Best in highly competitive markets where you must differentiate through a mechanism or system.
- Example of the form (finance): "You'll never believe what we found on page 36 of [well-known person]'s financial disclosure... a little-known type of income."
- Example (business education): "The best online business to start as a beginner." The audience already knew the popular alternatives; the lead positions a different one they have not considered, which sets up disqualifying the alternatives later.

### 6. Proclamation lead
Open with a forecast or declaration meant to startle: something big is about to happen and they will either lose a lot or miss a large gain. The aim is to disarm the reader just long enough to work back to the product. Forms: "[Asset class] extermination: why safe investors may lose everything within six months"; "I'm starting a revolution"; "The Digital Renaissance is here."
- Use to create problem awareness in people who have none, and to reach beyond an audience your direct claims have saturated.
- **A proclamation cannot be made up at the desk; it has to be found.** It comes out of extended research (history, economics, current events) and team brainstorming. The parallel is usually found first ("this period resembles ours") and the name comes later.
- It is almost always the Big Idea stated outright, so the Big Idea must be strong and its supporting facts real.

### 7. Story lead
The most indirect form, and potentially the most powerful: people think, learn and decide through stories, and once a story starts they need to know how it ends.
- Especially useful where the market distrusts big claims. A story does not trigger the same resistance.
- Sources: a strong testimonial, the expert's biography, historical proof, a track record. A whole promotion can be wrapped around one success story.
- Classic forms: "My doctor sent me to the nursing home to die. What happened next might be called a miracle." And the long-running newspaper subscription letter about two graduates with identical starts and very different outcomes -- a story built on a duality, where the only difference is the product.
- Requires skill to connect the story back to problem, solution and product without losing people.

## A3. Choosing

1. Identify the awareness level of the traffic this piece will actually get (warm list vs. cold recommended viewers).
2. Pick the lead type that matches it. More aware -> more direct. Less aware -> more indirect.
3. **One-way compatibility:** a lead built for a less-aware audience also works on a more-aware one, but not the other way round. If the audience is mixed, write for the least aware segment you need to convert.
4. Do not go more indirect than necessary. Risks of an unneeded or badly handled indirect lead:
   - it is too far from the product to connect back
   - it is too subtle, and takes too long to arrive
   - it bores people before the point
   - it follows something interesting but irrelevant, leaving the straight line toward the thesis
5. If you are unsure, or the writer is inexperienced, use problem-solution.
6. Lead with the theme the audience has already bought into. If they have already decided on the vehicle (e.g. "online business") and are stuck on *which one*, open on that question, not on the surface desire.

## A4. Lead Checklist

A lead must do four things:

1. **Expand on the Big Idea.** The Unique Proposition (see `longform-canvas.md`, step 3) is usually the core of the lead.
2. **Sell the viewership.** Give a reason to stay to the end: what they will learn, see or be shown ("...and on [date] I'll show you how").
3. **Set the stakes.** A reason to pay attention NOW ("there's a much bigger story here that isn't being told").
4. **Provide proof of the promise.** Needed when the claim concerns money or health, where scepticism is highest. Can be lighter when the opening is an intriguing narrative people will keep watching in order to see proven.

Length on video: keep the lead under two minutes and as short as it can be; two minutes is already long for retention.

Further mechanics that belong in the opening:
- **Open a loop** and close it late. Example: begin with a damaging admission ("for years I haven't been fully honest with you -- here's why"), move on, and resolve it near the end.
- **Start seeding** the duality and the need for the solution here; the first minutes are part of the sale.
- On video, the first 30 seconds decide retention (Part B). Confirm quickly that the video will deliver what the title promised.

# Part B -- Packaging & Distribution

## B1. Traffic Is the Bigger Lever

Most copywriters spend their effort trying to raise conversion on the same small audience. Lifting an already-tested sales video from 3% to 5% is very hard; it is the last 20% of improvement that eats 80% of the effort. Getting 10x or 100x more people to see the piece is often easier and worth far more: a video converting at a fraction of a percent with millions of views outsells a brilliant one with a few thousand.

So ask at the start: **how will this be packaged so the platform shows it to a very large audience?** A slightly lower conversion rate is an acceptable price for a much larger reach. More traffic makes conversion rate less critical.

This is also where a writer who understands platforms beats a technically better writer who does not.

## B2. Pick the Medium for What It Can Do

| Medium | What works there | Lifespan | Suitable for long-form selling? |
|--------|------------------|----------|--------------------------------|
| Instagram | Short video, stories. Long story sequences, multi-part carousels and long captions are not consumed | 1-3 days | Not natively. Use it to send people elsewhere. The one workable long format is a **live stream**, which people will watch for an hour with few distractions |
| Facebook | Longer text is tolerated, so offers can appear | About a week | Weak: a lot of production for a few days of exposure |
| Email / own website | Offers and long copy | While the campaign runs | Yes |
| YouTube | Long video | Months to years; old videos keep being recommended | **Best home for a VSL** -- one piece can sell for years |

Do not cut a long VSL into carousel pieces or post an offer where the platform's users do not consume offers. The quality of the copy cannot overcome a format the medium does not support.

## B3. How YouTube Distributes a Video

A new video is first shown to a small group who already know the channel. Depending on how many click and how long they watch, it is shown to a larger group, then a larger one, eventually to people who have never heard of you. It keeps expanding until performance drops. A video that keeps performing can keep growing for years.

Think of the title + thumbnail as an **ad** for the video, and the recommendation system as an ad auction you do not pay for. Two numbers decide the outcome:

**Click-through rate (CTR)**
- Aim for about 10% on day one and about 5% over the longer term.
- CTR falls naturally as the audience broadens; around 1% is normal for a video with millions of views.
- Driven by topic first, then title and thumbnail.

**Retention**
- Good is roughly 35-60% average viewed, depending on length (longer videos sit lower).
- Most drop-off happens in the first 30 seconds, so the lead is the main retention tool.
- Suggested VSL length for this purpose: 20-40 minutes. Beyond that, strong retention is hard to hold.

**They must work together.** High CTR with a steep early drop-off is read as clickbait and the video stops being recommended. The opening has to confirm and begin delivering the title's promise quickly. High CTR plus high retention gets pushed hard.

## B4. Package Before You Write

The topic sets the ceiling for CTR. You cannot write the script and then look for a clickable wrapper; decide the packaging first and write under that theme.

- **Choose a broad topic that still matches where the audience is.** "How to start a marketing agency" appeals only to people already interested in that specific solution. "The best online business model to start as a beginner" appeals to everyone considering an online business -- and matches the audience's actual question. The specific solution is then revealed inside the video.
- A niche method nobody has heard of cannot be the packaging. Package the broad question; make the niche method the answer.
- The sweet spot: a theme broad enough for large reach, under which the full belief chain can still be delivered and convert.

Procedure: (1) write the audience's philosophical problem as the question they are asking; (2) state it as a broad topic; (3) draft title and thumbnail concepts; (4) only then outline the script; (5) make sure the first 30 seconds pay off the title.

## B5. Titles

- Short, broad, punchy, straight to the point.
- Do not give the full context; leave a gap (mildly clickbait in form, honest in substance).
- Long direct-response headlines (three sentences, mechanism, specific promise) are excellent on a sales page and fail as YouTube titles. Keep them for the lead and the landing page.
- Study how large educational and entertainment channels title subjects most people consider dull.

## B6. Thumbnails

- One dominant focal point; 1-3 elements at most.
- No walls of text, no clutter.
- Plain stills usually beat heavy photo manipulation.
- Produce at least five per video and test them.

## B7. Iterate After Publishing

YouTube is forgiving: title and thumbnail can be changed at any time, any number of times, at no cost. If a new package lifts CTR, the video gets a fresh chance and can be recommended as if new, even years later. A flat video is not finished; re-package before re-scripting. A slow start is normal for evergreen pieces: the well-known example took a long while to gain traction and then grew exponentially.

## B8. Evergreen vs. Event-Based

| | Evergreen ("slow burner") | Event-based |
|--|---------------------------|-------------|
| Purpose | Sell an always-open offer every day for years | A sales spike over about a week, then taken down |
| Concept | Timeless: a question people asked five years ago and will ask in ten | Tied to what is happening right now |
| Title | Stable; update the year when it changes | Timely, trend-driven |
| View curve | Slow start, then compounding | Spike, then nothing |
| Typical lead | Promise, problem-solution, secret/system | Proclamation, story |

Decide which one you are writing before choosing the Big Idea. A current-events proclamation will date an evergreen VSL; a timeless how-to will not create a one-week surge.

## B9. Packaging Deliverables

When writing a sales video for YouTube, provide alongside the script:
- the broad topic and the audience question it answers
- 3-5 title options (short)
- 5 thumbnail concepts (focal point + up to two supporting elements)
- the first 30 seconds marked, showing where the title's promise is confirmed
- evergreen or event-based, and why
