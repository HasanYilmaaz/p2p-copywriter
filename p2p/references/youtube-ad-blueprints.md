# YouTube Ad Blueprints

Six proven ad skeletons, extracted by breaking down long-running, high-view YouTube ads (tens of millions of views each). Each is a numbered list of what every segment *does*, so you can plug in your own 5 Basic Elements. Most also work on Facebook/Instagram once adapted (faster pace, scroll-stopper first, short CTA -- see `advertising-rules.md` section 6).

Rules that apply to every blueprint are in `advertising-rules.md` section 5 (5-second hook, ~20-second filter, early CTA before ~60 seconds, never end on the CTA, long final CTA with a 7-10 second hold).

## Choosing a blueprint

| # | Blueprint | The ad sells | Best when |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Provoke-and-invite | Registration (free workshop, webinar, event) | Cold audience, a default life-path to attack, a presenter with results |
| B | Inescapable scenario + showcase | A premium product or course | Strong authority figure, high production value, something that can be demonstrated |
| C | One-technique tease | Curiosity (click to a VSL / "special video") | A named signature solution nobody can guess |
| D | Identity upgrade | A product (e-commerce) | Everyday product, brand with personality, viewers have never questioned the status quo |
| E | Problem-unaware product | A product (e-commerce) | Market does not know it has the problem; mechanism is easy to show |
| F | Looming scenario | Curiosity/desire (free presentation) | Fear-led angle, a real external development, heavyweight credentials |

When producing a batch, write the same offer through several blueprints: structural variety is what makes tests informative.

Bracketed words are slots. Quoted fragments are illustrations of sentence function, not text to reuse.

## A. Provoke-and-invite (free workshop / webinar)

Source pattern: a two-minute invitation to a free online workshop. The first CTA lands before the halfway point.

1. **Provoking question (0-5 s).** One short question that attacks the default plan, e.g. "Is this your life plan?"
2. **Duality.** Describe the default path so its absurdity shows: a lifetime of sacrifice in order to end up exactly where they started (live frugally for forty years so you can retire and live frugally). Most viewers have never seen their life framed that way; that is the conflict.
3. **Ask "why?"** Why would anyone choose this?
4. **Give the reason.** Because they have been told the alternative requires [big risk], [years], [genius]. Three limiting beliefs, stated fast.
5. **Who you are.** Name + one or two lines of what you have achieved.
6. **Allay the fears.** "It doesn't take any of those things." / "It doesn't have to be that way." This is the pivot into the solution.
7. **Early CTA seeded with a benefit.** "Don't take my word for it -- that's why I'm running a free [workshop] where we will [specific outcome]. To attend, click [where]." Short. Before ~60 s.
8. **Show what happens after the click.** Put the registration page on screen: "it takes you to a page like this, you register, done."
9. **Benefit while removing pain.** "[No upfront cost], [can work this week], [no technical skills]."
10. **Testimonials with honest framing.** Flash results on screen: "I can't guarantee you'll get these results, but you will see the exact steps these people used."
11. **Stack benefits.** Repeat and extend the promise for those who have not clicked. This segment exists to buy time and convert the unconvinced.
12. **Reason to act now.** A bonus for everyone who attends, it is free, it is today.
13. **Longer CTA.** Repeat exactly where to click, on mobile and on desktop. A touch of humour here keeps people watching while they decide.
14. **End-screen hold (7-10 s).** The headline/USP on screen with arrows pointing at the button. Nobody clicks after the ad ends.

## B. Inescapable scenario + product showcase (premium course / product)

Source pattern: a trailer for an expert-led online course. Long for an ad, because the production value justifies showing it off. No spoken CTA mid-ad; the button is on screen throughout.

1. **Inescapable statement (0-5 s).** "Everything in life is a [topic]." One flat sentence that removes the option of not caring and makes the viewer ask why. Even people with zero interest in the topic keep watching.
2. **Expand with everyday proof (to ~17-20 s).** Three or four mundane situations that are secretly the topic, then: "your life could be in a completely different place just by getting better at this." The duality is now explicit: *learn this or lose*. Anyone still uninterested skips here, uncharged.
3. **Third-party authority through visuals.** Rapid B-roll: news footage, an anchor introducing the expert, archive clips. Others establish who this is before the expert says a word about themselves.
4. **Role and purpose.** "My job was [role]; my purpose was to [goal]."
5. **Emotional appeal.** A universal human line connected to the topic (everyone deserves to be heard).
6. **What the product is.** "In my [product] I give you the [strategies] I developed as one of the top [role] in the world."
7. **What they will get and why it matters.** Three items, from basic to intriguing.
8. **Showcase: give a real taste.** Teach one small, usable technique in full and prove it with a real example. This is not "giving away content"; it lets the viewer experience owning the product. Show, do not claim.
9. **More of what is inside.** Brief.
10. **Why it matters to you.** Reframe the topic away from the common misconception (not win/lose, but collaboration) and tie it to improving the viewer's life. Aimed at those not yet convinced.
11. **Outro: name + brand.** "I'm [name], and this is my [product]."
12. **Time to click.** The ad keeps running on brand visuals.

Use the long showcase only when the product is impressive on camera. Otherwise shorten step 8.

## C. One-technique tease (curiosity ad to a VSL)

Source pattern: a talking-head ad whose only goal is the click to a "special video". Every sentence opens or widens an information gap.

1. **"If you ___" call-out (0-5 s).** "If a [target] wants [second chance at X] ..." -- a situation nearly everyone in the audience has lived (verify).
2. **"...or it's happening right now."** Same situation in the present tense. Adds urgency while the viewer still cannot skip.
3. **The promise of one technique.** "There is one simple [technique] any [target] can use to not just avoid that but flip the situation and get the exact opposite result."
4. **Tease its nature.** Innocent, simple, under the radar.
5. **First real step.** "First you [do this]..."
6. **A concrete, specific step.** Give something tangible and exact (the source gives a nine-word message to send). Specificity proves there is substance.
7. **"Then do you know what you do next?"**
8. **Most think they do.** "Most [targets] think they have the answer, but only [3 in 100] get the next step right."
9. **Disqualify the obvious answer.** "No, you don't [obvious move]."
10. **Why it is wrong.** "Because that is [X], and [X] is a huge mistake most [targets] make when [pursuing outcome] -- it causes [the opposite of what they want]."
11. **"The moment [result] happens -- and it will -- you do one simple thing."** Assumed success.
12. **Tease the benefits of that thing.** "Something that [benefit], something that [overrides objection], something that [deep desire]."
13. **Name the signature solution.** "The technique is called [name]."
14. **The qualifier.** "If you know what to do next, there's nothing more for us to say to each other. But if you don't..." Nobody can know, because the name is yours.
15. **CTA.** "...stop whatever you're doing and watch the [special video] at the link right now."
16. **Who you are.** "My name is [name], and I created [signature solution]."
17. **What the video reveals.** "You'll discover a secret about [topic] you've never heard before, and I'll reverse-engineer the process of [desired outcome]."
18. **"And here's the best part: even if..."** Objection handling that also lengthens the CTA: "even if you've already [mistake], even if you're in [worst case], it's not too late."
19. **Closing CTA.** "I can only show you [name] if you click and watch the video right now."

### Worked transposition (how to model sentence by sentence)

Steps 7-10 moved from the source niche to weight loss:

| Function | Skeleton | Weight-loss version |
|---|---|---|
| Question | Do you know what to do next? | Do you know what to do next? |
| Most think they do | Most [targets] think they have the answer, but only 3 in 100 get it right | Most women think they have the answer, but only 3 in 100 get the next step right |
| Disqualify | No, you don't [obvious move] | No, you don't go on a calorie-restricting diet |
| Reason | Because that is [X] | Because that slows down your metabolism |
| Mistake --> opposite result | [X] is a huge mistake most make; it causes [opposite] | Slowing your metabolism is a huge mistake most people make when trying to lose weight; it makes you gain more even though you eat less |

The connective tissue stays; every noun, claim and consequence is replaced. Step 14 only works if step 13 is a real signature solution (see `copy-formulas.md`): "go low-carb" lets the viewer think "I know that" and leave.

## D. Identity upgrade (e-commerce, brand with personality)

Source pattern: a humorous presenter-led ad for an everyday personal-care product. Humour is the brand's tone, not a requirement of the structure.

1. **Provocation + pattern interrupt (0-5 s).** "Listen up." Then a blunt verdict on the thing they use every day. Not what an ad is supposed to say.
2. **Verify.** "You've probably never even questioned [the thing]." True, so the message gets through the logic bubble.
3. **Most people still use the old way.** Framed as mildly embarrassing. Shaming normally raises guards; it only works here because the humour is obvious. Without humour, soften to "most people simply never look into it".
4. **"Is that what you want?"** Restate the unflattering identity.
5. **Introduce the product early as the upgrade.** "Now you can upgrade your [routine] with [product]."
6. **Who it is for: the aspirational identity.** "This is for [people] who [admirable trait], [admirable trait], [admirable trait]." Describe who they want to be, not the average buyer.
7. **Direct benefits.** Beyond the obvious one, add a second ("you won't just [A], your [B] will be [better]"). Defuse the objection the second benefit raises.
8. **Variants as "we've got you".** "You want [X]? [Variant]. You want [Y]? [Variant]." Rhythm and choice.
9. **"Now you're wondering: why have I been doing it wrong for so long?"** Answer with the enemy's story: when and why the mainstream version got worse (cheaper, faster, artificial).
10. **Why yours is better and how it delivers.** "The good news: we make ours with [what the enemy removed]."
11. **Benefit run.** Sensory, specific.
12. **"Still not sure?" Reviews.** Real customer reviews, delivered creatively.
13. **Guarantee.** Full satisfaction or it is on us.
14. **CTA + tagline + the alternative.** "Click for [product]. [Tagline]. Or don't, and stay [unflattering identity]."
15. **Post-CTA hold.** The presenter stays on screen and plays with the fact that the viewer still has not clicked. This is the "time to click" device done as entertainment.

## E. Problem-unaware product (e-commerce, mechanism demo)

Source pattern: an absurd-comedy ad for a bathroom posture product. The market did not know it had a problem; the structure fixes that. Considered the cleanest template for selling a product in an ad.

1. **Big pattern interrupt (0-5 s).** A surreal image and line that could not belong to a normal ad. Keep the interrupts coming for the first seconds.
2. **Lead with the problem.** Turn to the viewer: "You know who's bad at [thing]? You." Then explain *why* the problem happens (the cause, in one picture).
3. **What the problem leads to.** "Is that a problem? I don't know, is [serious consequence] a problem?" List the consequences. Stab, then twist: now the problem must be solved.
4. **Present the solution -- without the product.** "But what happens when you [do it the right way]? [Cause disappears]." The solution is a concept (a position, a method, a principle), not a brand.
5. **Introduce the product as the vehicle.** "Our bodies were made to [solution], and now there's a product that lets you [solution] in your own home. Introducing [product]."
6. **Address the obvious objection.** If the product sounds like a joke, too simple or too strange, say so and answer it. Only needed when the objection is predictable.
7. **Show how the product delivers the solution.** Demonstrate the mechanism (before/after, on/off). Showing, not telling.
8. **Benefits.** Complete result, faster, more comfortable, tucks out of sight / practical design.
9. **CTA.** "So if you're a [human who has this need], click to order today." In the source this comes late (well past 1:30) -- acceptable because the ad is selling a product and is exceptionally engaging.
10. **Third-party social proof.** Press, well-known endorsers, review counts.
11. **Long final CTA.**

The critical move is steps 4-5. Never open with the product; first make them want the solution, then offer the thing that makes it possible.

## F. Looming scenario (fear-led, free presentation)

Source pattern: a calm, authoritative talking-head ad from a financial publisher inviting viewers to a free presentation. Entirely "away from pain".

1. **"Have you heard of [unknown thing]?" (0-5 s).** Must be genuinely unfamiliar.
2. **"Most people haven't, but it could completely upend [their life]."**
3. **Exactly how it will happen.** Name the specific provision/mechanism and what it replaces. Specific, not "things are going to get bad".
4. **Concrete personal consequences.** Three or four tangible losses the viewer can picture: what could be tracked, what could be switched off, what they own becoming worthless. This is where abstraction kills the ad -- name the actual thing they lose.
5. **Personal stake of the expert.** "The most alarming development I've seen in my career."
6. **Credentials sized to the claim.** A long, heavy list (institutions advised, professions, books). A huge claim from a nobody is not believed.
7. **Media proof.** "Places like [outlets] ask me what's coming."
8. **Rapport.** "Just like you, I'm a [shared identity]."
9. **Privileged information.** "This prediction is based on my own research and my contacts in [inner circle]."
10. **Turn to hope.** "The good news is there are ways to protect yourself and your [asset]..." A fear ad must end on a way out.
11. **Throw rocks at the enemy.** "...from falling under the control of [enemy both dislike]." Now the speaker is on the viewer's side.
12. **Scenario urgency.** "Once it's fully implemented, it could be too late." The deadline is the world's, not the marketer's.
13. **Show the gun.** "And trials are already underway." Not "will happen" -- "is happening".
14. **The offer.** "That's why I put together a presentation on the steps to take before that happens."
15. **Scarcity on the content.** "It's free to watch, but I can't promise how long it will stay available."
16. **CTA pointing at the button**, then time to click.

Cautions: fear-led ads can be attacked as preying on fear, so use them deliberately and truthfully. This skeleton is YouTube-safe; on Facebook/Instagram most of steps 4 and 12 would need abstraction to pass policy.

## Breaking down a new ad yourself

When the user supplies a reference ad (or you have a transcript of one), build a new blueprint instead of forcing it into A-F:

1. Go sentence by sentence. For each, write what it is *doing*, not what it says. Vocabulary: provoke, pattern interrupt, verify, call out, duality, ask why, give reason, authority (self / third-party), rapport, allay fear, enemy, tease, concrete step, disqualify obvious answer, name signature solution, solution concept, product as vehicle, demo, showcase, benefit, benefit-minus-pain, testimonial, review, guarantee, objection, reason to act now, early CTA, long CTA, hold.
2. Note what is on screen at each step (B-roll, page preview, arrows, testimonials flashing).
3. Mark the timing landmarks: what happens by 5 s, by ~20 s, where the first CTA falls, how long the ad continues after the last CTA.
4. Find the duality. Write it in one line ("either ___ or ___"). If you cannot, look again -- it is there in ads that perform.
5. Number the list. That is the skeleton.
6. Fill each step with the user's target customer, desired outcome, benefit, pain point, signature solution and three-layer problems.
7. Read it aloud for pace; cut any sentence that does not move the viewer forward.
