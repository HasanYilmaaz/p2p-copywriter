# P2P Advertising Rules

How to write paid ads (YouTube, Facebook/Instagram) the Pen2Profit way. Read this before writing any ad. Companion files:

- `youtube-ad-blueprints.md` -- six proven YouTube ad skeletons, segment by segment, plus the method for modeling a new ad
- `landing-page-rules.md` -- the page the ad sends people to (headline criteria, ad scent, CTA button)
- `copy-formulas.md` -- 5 Basic Elements and the 6-Step Formula used inside every ad
- `persuasion-filters.md` -- allay fears, throw rocks at enemies, etc. (used as ad segments)

---

## 1. What changes from email to ads (and what does not)

**Does not change:** the audience, their problems (external / internal / philosophical), desires, hot buttons, angles, and the structures. An ad still verifies an experience, validates the internal problem, pivots to a philosophical problem through a duality, explains why, and gives the solution with a CTA.

**Changes:** pace and flow.

| | Email | Ad |
|---|---|---|
| Hard part | Getting the open | Keeping attention every second |
| Once started | Reader will probably finish | 80-90% will NOT watch; default behaviour is skip/scroll |
| Distractions | Almost none | Skip button, other posts, stories, suggested videos |
| Room to breathe | Long sentences, slow build are fine | None. Every sentence must earn its place |
| Cost of a miss | Nothing | Every tested variation costs money |

Consequences for the writer:

- **You cannot paste a winning email into an ad.** The angle can be reused; the sentences must be rewritten shorter, faster, and front-loaded.
- **Cut any sentence that does not need to be there.** One slack sentence is enough for the viewer to hit skip, because skipping costs one tap and the button is always visible.
- **Write for the eye as well as the ear.** A video ad has visuals; even if you are not the editor, script visual anchors, B-roll, on-screen text and what is shown at each beat.
- **Do not bring email habits** (slow openers, long setups, delayed point) into ad scripts.

Ads are harder than email. Expect more drafts and more variations.

## 2. Two questions to answer before writing a word

### Question 1: What am I really selling in this ad?

The ad's job is defined by where the click lands, not by the end product.

| Destination | What the ad sells | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| E-commerce product page | The product, i.e. the solution to a problem | The ad itself must sell: problem, solution concept, product as vehicle, benefits, proof, guarantee. Needs more time. |
| Event / webinar / workshop registration | The desire to attend, the value of being there | Sell what they will see and get by attending, and a reason to register now. Do not sell the paid product. |
| Lead magnet (PDF, quiz) | The desire to consume that free thing | Sell the answer the quiz/PDF gives them. Lower the opt-in fear. |
| VSL / "free training" / "free case study" / sales letter | **Curiosity** | The viewer does not know a sale is coming. Open an information gap the page will close. Tease, do not explain. |
| Local-business lead gen | The enquiry / the booking | Sell the next small step. |

Think of the YouTube homepage: every thumbnail is an ad selling curiosity about one video. A VSL ad works the same way.

The CTA always exists, but its content follows from this answer ("register", "take the quiz", "watch the video", "order today"). Getting this wrong is the most common strategic mistake: an ad that tries to sell the course when it only needs to sell the click.

### Question 2: Who will see it? (audience temperature)

| Temperature | Who | Objective | How to write |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold** | Never heard of you or the product | Prospect new people | Full structure: verify --> validate internal problem --> philosophical problem with a duality --> explanation --> solution --> CTA. This sequence is what builds enough rapport for a stranger to act. |
| **Warm** | Know you; clicked or visited a while ago; have not bought | Re-engage, not prospect | Skip the long build. Go straight to the point: remind them of the problem, the product, how it solves the problem. |
| **Hot** | Visited in roughly the last 7 days, close to the point of sale, may have bought before | Give the final push | Offers, promotions, urgency, scarcity. Best place for testimonials and social proof: they supply the missing credibility and reassurance. |

Different objective means different copy. Never write one ad "for everyone".

## 3. The working method: volume plus modeling

**The name of the game is volume.** One email is one email. One winning ad usually comes out of 10, 20, 50 or more tested variations. A writer who can only follow one structure runs out of variations fast.

**Originality is a risk when money is on the line.** Each test costs ad spend and clients want results quickly. Running ads is buying data; a competitor's long-running, high-view ad is data someone else already paid for. So:

1. Collect ads that are clearly working (high views, long-running, repeated many times).
2. Break each into building blocks: what is each sentence *doing*? (provoking, presenting a duality, verifying, teasing, giving a reason, allaying a fear, seeding a CTA...)
3. Write the block list as a numbered skeleton.
4. Plug your own 5 Basic Elements, problems and signature solution into the skeleton.
5. Repeat across many source ads to get many structurally different variations.

A realistic batch for a campaign: around 20 ads, roughly half on the standard 6-step structure and half or more modeled on proven ads (your own past winners or other brands').

**Model, do not copy.** Keep the skeleton and the function of each sentence; replace the content with your niche. Phrases that are pure structure ("Do you know what to do next?", "And here's the best part") can stay close to the source because they are proven; claims, names, examples and stories must be yours. See `youtube-ad-blueprints.md` for six ready skeletons and a worked transposition.

**Test new ideas only after something is working.** Until there is a winner, stick to proven principles.

**What every strong ad has in common:** a duality. However different the skeletons look, the high performers all build on a conflict (work your whole life to retire frugal; negotiate or lose; get paid without doing the work; protect yourself or lose your freedom). If a draft has no duality, it is not finished.

### Finding ads to model

- **YouTube ad library tool (VidTao, free tier):** search by channel or topic. Useful trick: search by CTA phrase ("learn more", "watch now", "download", "get this now") to surface direct-response ads you did not know existed. Sort by view count.
- **Stats-for-nerds trick:** when YouTube serves you a good ad, right-click it --> Stats for nerds --> copy the video ID --> paste it after `watch?v=` in the URL. You get the ad as a normal video with its view count and a link you can save. Works on mobile via the gear icon (may need enabling in settings).
- **Meta Ad Library:** search a niche keyword. An ad that appears over and over in many versions is working.
- **Keep a swipe file** of saved ads and go back to it every time you write.

## 4. Platform dynamics (why the rules differ)

| Dimension | YouTube (in-stream) | Facebook / Instagram |
|---|---|---|
| Viewer state | Clicked a video they want to watch; your ad is in the way | Scrolling a feed or tapping through stories; nothing forces a stop |
| Forced attention | 5 unskippable seconds | None in feed; 1-2 seconds in stories |
| First objective | Keep them past the 5-second mark | Stop the scroll |
| Biggest drop-off | Within the first ~30 seconds | Within the first 2-3 seconds; second biggest at the very end |
| Billing leverage | Early skips are not charged (course figure: before ~20 seconds) | Better average retention = cheaper CPM |
| After the ad ends | Viewer's video starts ~3 seconds later; chance to click is gone | Ad stays on screen until the viewer moves on |
| CTA | Early weak CTA + long, extended final CTA | Short CTA; do not drag the ending |
| Pace | Faster than normal YouTube content | Faster still; frequent shot changes |
| Policy | Broad, lenient; rejections rare, bans rarer | Strict; rejections common, a few rejections can kill the account |
| Formats | Video | Video (usually wins) and image + text |

The same script is sometimes run on both platforms, but for best performance adapt it to each column above.

## 5. YouTube ad rules

### 5.1 The first 5 seconds: sell the viewership

The viewer wants their video, not yours. In the 5 unskippable seconds the only goal is to make them not press skip. The first sentence must elicit an emotion and create intrigue. Proven openers:

- **Provoking question** -- "Is this your life plan?" followed by an unflattering summary of the default path.
- **Inescapable statement** -- a flat claim that puts everyone inside the topic ("Everything in life is a ___"), which also makes the viewer ask "why?".
- **"If you ___" call-out** -- name a situation the target has lived, then sharpen it to "...or it is happening right now" for urgency.
- **Pattern interrupt** -- say or show something nobody expects from an ad (blunt insult to the status quo, absurd visual).
- **"Have you heard of this?"** -- only works when the thing is genuinely unknown. Asking it about something everyone knows kills the hook.

### 5.2 Seconds 5-20: filter the audience

Skipped views in the opening stretch are not charged (the course cites roughly the first 20 seconds; exact billing thresholds are the platform's and can change -- the principle holds). Use it:

- Hook first (so targets stay past 5 seconds), then make it unmistakable who and what this is for before ~20 seconds.
- Non-targets skip and cost nothing; targets self-select. Example: a product for people with diabetes says so immediately.
- Do not try to keep everyone. A vague opening that retains non-buyers wastes budget.

### 5.3 CTA rules (the most common YouTube mistake)

- **Seed an early, weak CTA -- ideally before the 60-second mark,** often before the halfway point. It can be one line ("if you want to see how, click the link") possibly with one benefit attached. Its job is to tell the viewer an action exists.
- **Never end the ad on the CTA.** When the ad ends, the viewer's video starts about 3 seconds later. They need time to process that there is an action to take and then time to click. People click while the ad is still playing; once it ends you have lost them.
- **Keep going after the early CTA:** more problem, more solution, benefits, proof, objections. Everything after the first CTA is aimed at the people who have not clicked yet. The clickable button is on screen the whole time, so many have already left for the page.
- **Close with a longer, comprehensive CTA** and then hold: leave 7-10 seconds of the promise/USP on screen with visual pointers to the button, or keep the presenter on screen playing with the fact that the viewer has not clicked yet. The course reports this alone can lift performance several-fold (figures of 5-10x are cited).
- **Point at the button** verbally and visually.
- **Exception -- e-commerce:** when the ad itself must sell a product, the first CTA can come later (one example places it well past 1:30) because selling needs time, provided the ad is highly engaging. For lead gen, event or curiosity ads, do not wait that long.

### 5.4 Segments worth using in most YouTube ads

| Segment | What to do | Why it works |
|---|---|---|
| Duality | State the conflict early and keep returning to it | Conflict creates the story in the viewer's head; it is what holds attention |
| "Why?" + reason | Ask why they are stuck, then answer with what they have been told/believe | Moves blame to a belief and opens the door for a new one |
| Who you are | One or two lines of credentials, after the hook, never before | Earned attention first; authority second |
| Third-party authority | News clips, press logos, "featured in", quick B-roll | Authority given by others is stronger than authority you claim |
| Allay fears | "It doesn't have to be that way" | Creates the pivot into the solution |
| Show the next page | Show the landing page / what happens after the click | People avoid the unknown. Showing the page usually lifts clicks and, more importantly, lifts the completion rate on the page |
| Benefit minus pain | "No upfront cost, no tech skills, can work this week" | Removes the objection while stating the benefit |
| Testimonials with honest framing | "I can't guarantee your results, but you will see exactly what these people did" | Proof without an unbelievable (or non-compliant) guarantee |
| Showcase the product | Give a real taste: teach one technique, demo the mechanism | Showing beats telling. Worth extra length when production value is high; skip if the product has nothing impressive to show |
| Benefit stack | Benefit after benefit after the early CTA | Buys clicking time and converts the unconvinced |
| Reason to act now | Bonus for attendees, limited availability, or an external scenario | People procrastinate by default; without a reason for now they choose later |
| "Even if" objections | "Even if you've already ___, it's not too late" | Lengthens the CTA while handling objections |
| Reviews / guarantee | Customer reviews, satisfaction guarantee near the end | Final risk reversal for product ads |

### 5.5 Principles the breakdowns keep repeating

- **Product = vehicle, not solution.** In a product ad, first explain the solution to the problem (the concept), then introduce the product as the thing that lets them experience that solution. Then *show* how it does so.
- **Stab, then twist.** Name the problem, explain why it happens, then spell out what it leads to if left alone, so that not fixing it becomes unacceptable.
- **Concrete beats abstract.** "Dark forces want to control you" is weak; "your account could be switched off outside your control" is strong. Saying "you are in danger" is weaker than showing the gun: "trials are already underway". If a fear or promise is not landing, the usual cause is abstraction.
- **Every claim needs a proof point; bigger claim, bigger proof.** Do not move to the next claim until the current one is backed (credentials, third parties, mechanism, demonstration), or you lose believers along the way.
- **Two emotional directions:** away from pain/fear or toward pleasure. Fear-led ads are powerful but can backfire as "preying on fear" -- use carefully and always turn to "the good news is there is a way out".
- **Scenario-based urgency is the strongest urgency.** Instead of "offer ends Friday", the urgency comes from something happening in the world regardless of what the viewer does; waiting means living the pain.
- **Speak to who they want to be.** "This is for the kind of person who ___" describing the aspirational identity, not the average customer.
- **Verification can be tiny.** "You've probably never even questioned ___" works as a verify step because it is true of the viewer.
- **Rapport and common enemy.** "Just like you, I ___" plus an enemy both sides dislike puts the speaker on the viewer's side.
- **Privileged information.** Implying access to what others do not know plays on the wish to be in on the secret.
- **Naming the solution keeps the gap open.** "If you know what to do next, we have nothing more to say" only works when the answer is a named signature solution nobody can guess. A generic answer ("go low-carb") closes the gap.
- **Disqualify the obvious answer.** State what the viewer assumes the next step is, say it is the mistake most people make, and that it produces the opposite of what they want.
- **Shaming is dangerous.** Implying the viewer is foolish raises their guard. It only works wrapped in obvious humour that fits the brand.
- **Humour is optional.** You can model a humorous ad's structure without its tone.
- **Laziness sells -- when true.** "You don't have to do the hard thing" performs extremely well, but only use it when the result really is achievable without that work.
- **Match the market's awareness.** Some markets do not know they have a problem; some know the problem but not the solution; some know every solution. A problem-unaware market needs the ad to establish the problem first.

## 6. Facebook / Instagram ad rules

"Facebook ads" means both networks: feed, stories and reels are served from one ad system. Nearly all spend lands in feeds and stories, so every ad must work in both: story-friendly and feed-friendly.

Everything in section 5.4-5.5 applies. Only the differences follow.

### 6.1 The three jobs, in order

1. **Stop the scroll.** Nothing forces attention. Use scroll-stopping elements: striking or odd visuals, a physical prop that dramatises the problem, an abrupt random cut, or a direct line such as "If ___, stop scrolling for a second and listen." The feed is so crowded that successful ads are often almost obnoxious. That extreme is an option to test, not a requirement.
2. **Hook within 2-3 seconds.** This is where most viewers are lost, far earlier than on YouTube. The claim/marketing hook must be sharper and immediate; its job is to sell watching the rest.
3. **Keep it fast.** Faster than a YouTube ad. Frequent shot changes and visual anchors keep re-hooking the viewer; in this format the visuals matter almost as much as the words.

### 6.2 Video ad shape

Stop-scroll opener (often with a call-out of who it is for) --> name the common wrong approaches ("it's not X, not Y, and definitely not Z") --> duality ("what's the point of ___ if ___?") --> the alternative, framed as easier/sustainable --> the free thing on the other side of the click and what they will get from it --> short CTA.

Useful details from the breakdowns:

- Handle the opt-in objection inside the CTA when true: say plainly they will not be asked for an email / it takes six questions / it is free.
- State how small the ask is ("a 6-question quiz"), as the analysed ads do.
- For video ads, the headline, description and button text matter much less than the script.

### 6.3 CTA: short

The ad does not get replaced by other content when it ends; the viewer has to leave manually. So no extended CTA is needed, and a long one hurts: viewers drop when they sense the ending, average retention falls, and lower retention means more expensive CPMs. Say the CTA once, clearly, and end. This is the opposite of YouTube.

### 6.4 Image ads

When the ad only sells curiosity or desire (the page closes the gap), a video is not required:

- one scroll-stopping image,
- very short, simple copy,
- a quick CTA.

**Headline: keep it to one line.** A two-line headline pushes the description (the sub-headline with more room) out of view; one line keeps both visible and looks cleaner.

### 6.5 Policy (treat as a hard constraint)

Rejected ads are common and a handful of rejections can shut down the ad account -- a disaster for the client. Read the platform's current ad policies before writing for a sensitive niche. The patterns that trigger review:

- **Niche trigger words** -- e.g. the literal words "weight loss" in a weight-loss ad.
- **Money claims** -- any income claim, especially specific amounts.
- **Personal attributes** -- addressing the viewer as having a condition or state: "you're experiencing back pain", "you feel like you've gained weight". You cannot speak to the customer as directly as on YouTube.
- **Health claims.**
- **Results-based claims.**

**The policy test:** *Is this statement true for every single person who sees the ad?* "Do this and you will lose weight" is not, so the platform treats it as misleading.

**How to write inside the rules:**

- **Abstraction on purpose.** Normally concrete beats abstract; here you often must talk about the thing without naming the thing.
- **Analogies, verbal and visual.** Restricted niches communicate through objects and phrases that stand in for the subject.
- **Symbols for flagged words.** e.g. a money emoji instead of the word.
- **Third-person framing** (inferred from the course's own compliant headline, not stated as a rule). Describe what a group of people is doing rather than telling the viewer what they are or will get.
- **Do not model compliance on huge advertisers.** Big spenders have platform contacts who approve ads that would get a normal account rejected. Their structure is safe to model; their directness is not.

**The landing page is reviewed too.** Platform crawlers read the page; a compliant ad pointing at a non-compliant page is rejected just the same. See `landing-page-rules.md`.

## 7. Ad scent

Whoever clicks has an expectation created by the ad's promise and CTA. The page must fulfil exactly that: same promise, same words, same offer, same event/quiz/video name. If the ad mentions a seasonal sale, the page mentions it. Most of a page's conversion rate comes from this congruence. When you write an ad, always state what the page headline must say to keep the scent (details in `landing-page-rules.md`).

## 8. Pre-flight checklist

Run every ad script through this before delivering:

1. Is it clear what this ad sells (product / registration / desire / curiosity) and does the CTA match?
2. Is it written for one temperature?
3. Does the first sentence provoke, question or interrupt -- and would a stranger feel something?
4. Is there a duality, and does the ad keep coming back to it?
5. Could any sentence be removed without loss? Remove it.
6. Is the language concrete where policy allows?
7. Does each claim have a proof point of matching size?
8. **YouTube:** audience filtered before ~20 s? Early CTA before ~60 s? Content continues after it? Long final CTA plus 7-10 s hold with pointers?
9. **Facebook/Instagram:** scroll-stopper in the first frame? Hook inside 3 s? Fast cuts scripted? CTA short? Every line passes "true for every viewer"? No personal attributes, money/health/results claims, trigger words?
10. Is there a reason to act now?
11. Does the landing page say the same thing as the ad?
12. Are there enough variations (different hooks, different skeletons) to test?
