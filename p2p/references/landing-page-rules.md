# P2P Landing Page Rules

How to write the page an ad sends people to. The course teaches the most basic and most common type -- the **opt-in page** (register for an event or webinar, watch a free case study / VSL, download a lead magnet). The same elements and criteria carry over to other landing pages.

Related: `advertising-rules.md` (what the ad promised, platform policy), `copy-formulas.md` (5 Basic Elements, signature solution), `turkish-headline-formulas.md` (headline ideation in Turkish).

---

## 1. What the page is for

An opt-in page has three jobs, in this order of importance:

1. **Fulfil the ad's promise** (ad scent, section 4). The visitor arrives with an expectation; meet it instantly.
2. **Sell the registration** -- not the product. The visitor is deciding whether to give their details or click the button, nothing more. Copy that starts selling the paid offer here is solving the wrong problem.
3. **Sell the consumption.** A registration that never watches, attends or downloads is worth little. The page should also make them *want to consume* what they opted in for.

---

## 2. Anatomy

A basic opt-in page is deliberately small:

| # | Element | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Headline** | Does about 80% of the work. The promise, with a duality. |
| 2 | **Sub-headline** | Supports the headline: proof, mechanism, what makes it new. |
| 3 | **Image / video thumbnail** | Shows what they are about to get. |
| 4 | **CTA button** | The single action, phrased in the reader's voice. |
| 5 | **Third line (optional)** | A further short line whose job is to make them want to actually watch/attend -- selling the viewership -- and/or to carry the proof point. |

Between them, the supporting lines (2 and 5) must cover three things the headline usually cannot hold alone: **third-party proof**, **a named mechanism**, and **a reason to consume now**. How you distribute those across the two lines is flexible.

A VSL page is often even barer: a big headline above the video and nothing else, with the button appearing only after a set watch time (or a full sales page unfolding below).

---

## 3. Why the headline gets most of the effort

Work the numbers for the user when it helps them prioritise:

- Cost per click: $1. Page converts at 20% (a normal figure for cold traffic; 20-25%, sometimes lower).
- Five clicks per registration --> **$5 per lead**.
- New headline lifts conversion to 40% --> **$2.50 per lead**. Same ads, same budget, double the leads.

Getting the same halving from the ad side means writing an ad twice as good as one that already works, which is very hard. Getting it from a headline test is comparatively easy: headline changes alone can move page conversion by 100%, 200%, even 500%. The headline is the cheapest big lever in the funnel, so **always deliver several headline variants to test**, not one.

---

## 4. Ad scent (congruence)

Every click carries an expectation set by the ad and its CTA. If the page shows something else -- different promise, different wording, different offer -- the visitor concludes "this is not what I clicked for" and leaves without converting. The page is the fulfilment of the ad's call to action.

- Mirror the ad's promise and key phrases in the headline.
- Use the same name for the thing (the quiz, the workshop, the case study).
- If the ad mentions a promotion or occasion (e.g. a seasonal sale), the page must mention it too.
- If the ad said "no email required" or "six questions", the page must behave that way.
- If the ad showed the page on screen, the real page must look like that.

Most of a page's conversion rate comes from this congruence. So before writing, get the ad copy. If there is no ad yet, write the page promise first and tell the user what the ad must say.

## 5. The 8 headline criteria

Run every headline (together with its supporting lines) through all eight. Treat each as a yes/no gate with a stated reason, not a vague score.

**Running example** (an opt-in page for a free case study, cold audience of young adults; money word replaced by an emoji for policy reasons):

> *How 20-year-olds are using this beginner-friendly business model to get 💰 from small businesses without having to deliver any of the work themselves*
> Supporting line: *the same strategy used by companies like [two household-name platforms] -- experts are calling it the new [named] economy*

| # | Criterion | The test | How the example passes | How to engineer it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Appeals to self-interest** | Does the reader see at once what is in it for them? | They want to learn how peers are getting paid | Lead with the desired outcome of the target customer, in their words |
| 2 | **Implies proof of promise** | Does the page already suggest the promise is real? | Household-name companies use the same strategy; "experts are calling it..." | Borrow third-party sources; name a mechanism so the promise has a "how" |
| 3 | **Gives hope** | Can the reader see people like themselves succeeding? | A 20-year-old reads that other 20-year-olds are doing it | Name the target group as the subject of the headline ("How [people like you] are...") |
| 4 | **Creates tension** | Is there a duality? | Getting paid **without** doing the work | Pair the outcome with the removal of the expected cost: "[outcome] without [pain point]" |
| 5 | **Piques intellectual interest** | Does it sound new, something they have not heard? | A "new economy" label; a secret hidden in plain sight inside famous companies | Give the mechanism a name; frame it as the best-kept secret hiding in plain sight |
| 6 | **Triggers the dominant emotion** | Which emotion does it fire? | Greed first; laziness second | Decide the direction (toward pleasure or away from fear) and the emotion (greed and fear are the two strongest) before writing; a headline with no emotional response fails |
| 7 | **Is believable** | Would a sceptic accept it? | Big claim, but backed by big companies and expert naming | The bigger the claim, the bigger the proof it needs. Shrink the claim or raise the proof. "Lose 20 kilos in one day" fails no matter how exciting |
| 8 | **Is dramatized** | Is the language vivid rather than flat? | It could have been said in six flat words; it was not | Add specifics, contrast and concrete detail; choose the dramatic phrasing that is still true |

Notes on applying them:

- **Criteria pull against each other.** 5, 6 and 8 push the claim up; 7 pulls it down. The resolution is always proof (criterion 2), not a timid claim.
- **Believability is not optional.** Big claims alone do not convert; believed claims do.
- **The duality is the engine.** "[Outcome] without [the thing they assume is required]" is the most reliable way to pass criterion 4, and it usually helps 1 and 6 as well.
- **"Beginner-friendly", "simple", "even if..."** qualifiers serve hope and laziness; use them when true.
- **A landing page headline may be long.** The example is a full sentence carrying group, mechanism, outcome and pain removal. (A Facebook ad headline is different: keep that to one line.)

### Headline shapes the course demonstrates

- `How [target group] are using [this new/simple mechanism] to [desired outcome] without [pain point]`
- Supporting line: `The same [strategy/method] used by [credible third parties] -- [experts/insiders] are calling it [named mechanism]`

For more shapes, especially in Turkish, see `turkish-headline-formulas.md`; whichever formula you start from, it still has to pass the eight gates.

## 6. CTA button

- **Use action words:** watch, get, register, download, start -- plus "now" where natural.
- **Write it as the reader speaking.** First-person buttons have tested better than bare commands: "I want to watch the case study now" beats "Watch the case study now".
- Pattern: `I want to [action] [the thing] now`.
- The button must name the same thing the ad's CTA named (ad scent).

---

## 7. Policy: the page is reviewed like the ad

Ad platforms run automated crawlers over the destination page. A fully compliant ad pointing at a non-compliant page still gets rejected, and repeated rejections can get the ad account banned. So every Facebook/Instagram restriction applies to page copy when that traffic is used (and Google/YouTube review pages as well):

- no niche trigger words, income or money claims, health claims, results guarantees;
- no addressing the reader by personal attribute ("you are overweight", "you are in debt");
- test each claim with: *is this true for every single person who reads it?*

Ways to keep the power while staying inside the rules:

- replace a flagged word with a symbol (the example uses 💰 instead of "money"/"paid");
- describe what a group of people is doing ("how 20-year-olds are...") rather than promising the reader a result;
- use abstraction and analogy for the sensitive part and keep the rest concrete.

Ask the user which platform sends the traffic; write to the strictest one in play.

## 8. Process

1. **Collect inputs:** page type and the one action; traffic source and temperature; the ad copy; the 5 Basic Elements; available proof (third parties, numbers, press, mechanism name).
2. **Fix the promise** from the ad (scent). One sentence.
3. **Pick the dominant emotion and the duality.**
4. **Draft many headlines**, using different shapes.
5. **Gate them** through the 8 criteria. Fix failures: add proof for believability, add the "without" for tension, name the mechanism for novelty, name the group for hope.
6. **Keep the best 3-5** as test variants; recommend one and say why.
7. **Write the supporting lines** so that proof, mechanism and reason-to-consume are all present.
8. **Write the button** in first person.
9. **Describe the visual** (what the image or thumbnail shows).
10. **Policy pass** for the traffic platform.
11. **Scent check** against the ad, line by line.
12. **Recommend testing headlines first** -- it is the highest-leverage test on the page.

## 9. Common mistakes

- Selling the product or the price on an opt-in page.
- A headline that does not repeat what the ad promised.
- Big claim, no proof point anywhere on the page.
- No duality: a plain benefit statement with nothing to resolve.
- Flat, generic phrasing ("Learn how to grow your business").
- A command-style or vague button ("Submit", "Learn more").
- Compliant ad, non-compliant page.
- Delivering a single headline with nothing to test against.
- Forgetting to make them want to watch/attend after registering.
