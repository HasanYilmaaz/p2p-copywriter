# P2P Core Principles

These are the beliefs every piece of P2P copy is built on. They are written as operating rules: what to do, why it works, and how to check yourself. When a rule seems not to fit a situation, fall back on the reason behind it.

Related references:
- `research-framework.md` -- how to uncover the problems these principles tell you to lead with
- `emotions-and-concreteness.md` -- pain/pleasure framing, rock bottom vs. top of the mountain, the abstraction filter
- `ai-research-prompts.md` -- prompt templates for the research steps

## 1. The #1 Job: Understand the Reader Better Than They Understand Themselves

Copy quality is not decided by the words. It is decided by how deeply you understand the reader's problem and how well you extracted it before writing.

- The structure of a piece is roughly the same every time. What changes the result is the raw material you plug in: the more real and specific the problems, the better the copy.
- When you can describe someone's situation better than they can describe it themselves, they conclude on their own: "this person really gets me, so they must know how to fix it." At that point you stop being one option among many and become the only viable solution.
- That reaction is called **visceral desire**: the reader wants the solution so much that they start building their own reasons to buy.
- So spend most of your time on research and problem analysis, and only then write. If you have a fixed amount of time, the majority goes to "sharpening the axe". Once the research is done, writing is filling in a structure.

**Self-check:** Could the reader have written this sentence about themselves? If the copy only says what any competitor could say ("lose weight fast"), you have not done the job yet.

## 2. Aware vs. Unaware Problems (Symptoms vs. the Underlying Problem)

People have problems they know about and problems they do not know about.

- The pain points a prospect can list are usually **symptoms**. Beneath them sits one underlying problem that causes several of those symptoms.
- The most valuable move in copy is to connect two things the reader had never connected ("the reason your work performance dropped is the way you judge your worth by your weight") and show them the hidden cause. That is an epiphany moment, and it is what creates visceral desire.
- Whoever makes the reader aware of the problem owns it. Every later time the reader runs into that pain in daily life, they are reminded of the person who named it. This is why you choose problems that occur *often* (see `research-framework.md`).
- Once a reader has seen the underlying problem, they cannot unsee it. They can no longer just live with it.

## 3. Stab, Then Twist the Knife -- Then Offer the Way Out

To solve the reader's problem you first have to make them feel it.

- Naming the problem is the stab. Describing how it feels and what it is costing them in daily life is the twist. The deeper the pain is felt, the more likely they are to look for a solution.
- Rule of thumb: the better you are at pinpointing the problem *and* making the reader feel it, the higher the conversion rate.
- Only after the pain is felt do you present the solution. A solution offered to someone who does not feel the problem is just information.
- Always turn toward the positive afterwards ("but it doesn't have to be that way"). Pain is the lead, not the whole message. See the ethics note in `emotions-and-concreteness.md`.

## 4. Emotion First, Logic Second

Every sale, cheap or expensive, is made emotionally and then justified with logic.

- "Emotion" here means the story the reader tells themselves. People make decisions that look irrational from the outside because an internal story made the decision feel right.
- The logical argument is still required. The reader uses it to justify the decision *to themselves* (not mainly to others). Without it the emotional pull has nothing to stand on.
- Order matters: elicit the emotion (lead with the problem at the internal level), then supply the reasons why this solution makes sense, why at this price, why now.
- A beautiful, moving piece with no logical case fails on the second half. A list of rational benefits with no felt problem fails on the first half.

## 5. Communicate at the Deep Layers, Not the Surface

Most copy fails to trigger emotion because it speaks only at the first, shallow layer of the problem.

| Layer | What it is | Level of communication |
|-------|-----------|------------------------|
| External problem | What an outside observer would see | Rational |
| Internal problem | How the external problem makes them feel | Emotional -- this is the hook |
| Philosophical problem | The conflict the feeling creates inside them | Creates movement and decision |

"Lose 3 kg of belly fat in 45 days" is acceptable copy but shallow. "Do you feel trapped in a body you don't recognize?" speaks at the internal level. The stated desire ("lose 3 kg", "$10K a month") is never the real desire; the real one is found by going down the layers. Full procedure: `research-framework.md`.

## 6. Theory of Dualities (FOUNDATIONAL)

> Duality creates conflict --> conflict creates movement --> movement breeds decision.

- People do not decide because of arguments. They decide because of a story that forms in their own head, and stories form when two opposing things are set against each other.
- Your role is the **instigator**: put one thing against the other and ask "which one are you? what are you going to do about it?" Then stop. You do not need to resolve the conflict -- an unresolved conflict is uncomfortable, and the reader resolves it themselves. That is what replaces "convincing" and most objection handling.
- The common belief that one clever line late in a sales message overcomes an objection and flips the reader is wrong. What actually happens: you make them aware of a conflict, and they build their own case for buying.
- A duality is only as strong as its negative side. A hero needs a villain at least as strong as the hero. If the problem is described weakly, the solution and the desire feel weak too.
- Dualities nest: a big one can carry a whole promotion (its "big idea") with smaller ones inside each section.

Dualities you already have after research:
- Rock bottom vs. top of the mountain (named, e.g. "prisoner of the plate" vs. "picture-perfect fit")
- Problem vs. solution, problem vs. desire
- Stated (shallow) desire vs. real desire
- External vs. internal problem, internal vs. philosophical problem
- Conventional path vs. unconventional path (the engine of every contrarian angle: "the common approach doesn't work, do this instead")
- Thing-as-enemy vs. thing-as-tool (food as enemy vs. food as nourishment)

Reusable pattern:

```
Are you going to keep [negative side / conventional path] forever,
or are you ready to [positive side / desired result]?
If you're finally ready to [desire] without [pain point], I have an invitation for you.
```

Once you think in dualities you always know what to say next, because each section simply develops one side against the other. Habit to build: after any research session, go back over the material and list every duality you can spot, then reuse them.

## 7. Prosecutor Mindset: The Chain of Beliefs

Approach copy like a prosecutor proving a case to a jury, not like a storyteller.

- Before writing, ask: *What must the reader already believe, at the moment I present the product, to accept that it is the only sensible solution?*
- Then work backwards. For each belief ask what has to be believed before it. (To accept "he fired the shot" the jury must accept "he held the gun", before that "he was in the room", before that "he was not somewhere else".)
- The result is a chain. Write the copy so the reader walks it link by link. Never skip a link: a claim that rests on a belief the reader does not hold yet gets rejected, and everything after it falls.
- The end of the chain is two beliefs: "this *solution* is what I need" and then "this *product* is the way to get that solution".

## 8. The Self-Sealing Logic Bubble

Everyone sees a partial view of reality (the blind men each touching a different part of the elephant). Nobody is simply wrong; each view is limited -- and different. Assuming the reader sees the world the way you do is one of the most common mistakes.

Each person stands on a pyramid built from the ground (reality) up:

1. Experiences and observations
2. Assumptions
3. Conclusions
4. Beliefs -- these guide every decision

Around the top of the pyramid is a bubble. Inside it is "the obvious". Any incoming claim is tested with one question: *does this match my experiences, assumptions, conclusions and beliefs?* If not, it bounces off. That is why the logic is self-sealing.

**Three steps:**
1. **Map their pyramid.** What have they experienced, what do they assume, what have they concluded, what do they believe now? (This is what research produces.)
2. **Meet them where they are.** Open with something already inside the bubble: an experience they have had or a belief they already hold. A described experience they recognise ("every time you take a group photo, the first thing you notice is...") passes the bubble untouched.
3. **Install new beliefs one at a time**, each resting on the previous, until the chain from section 7 is complete.

**Golden rule:** do not hand people conclusions. Plant the seeds and let them reach the conclusion themselves, so it feels like their own idea. A conclusion the reader reached alone is defended; a conclusion handed over is examined.

Practical consequence: "this is the best protocol because X, Y, Z" fails if the reader does not already believe X, Y and Z. Start one step earlier.

## 9. Simplicity Is Everything

- If the reader has to think to get the message, the message is bad.
- Write so a 10-year-old would understand. Someone who writes for 10-year-olds beats someone who writes for 30-year-olds.
- People do not buy the best-written offer. They buy the idea that is easiest to understand.
- Complex vocabulary and elegant sentences pull attention to the writing and away from the message. What fancy prose really says is "look how well I write". The reader should notice what you say, never how you say it.
- A top-grade school essay is terrible copy. A simple one-two combination beats a spinning kick: plain sentences that land beat impressive ones that occasionally land.
- Write as if you were speaking to one person in conversation. Grammar matters only as far as it affects understanding; let a grammar checker clean up, and keep your attention on the message.
- **One message, one interpretation.** If 100 people read it, all 100 should arrive at the same conclusion. Abstract words break this rule because each reader fills them in differently; run every draft through the abstraction filter in `emotions-and-concreteness.md`.

## 10. Direct Response, Not Brand Awareness

| Brand awareness (not us) | Direct response (us) |
|--------------------------|----------------------|
| Creative, cinematic, often never mentions the product | Logical argument plus emotional trigger |
| Success = how many people saw it | Success = how many people took the desired action |
| Cannot tie spend to sales or compare one ad to another | Every piece is tracked to a response |
| Originality is the goal | Proven structures are the goal |

- The more original a piece is, the more unknown variables it carries, and the weaker its link to sales (the "we sell or else" school). Creativity means originality, and originality means untested.
- Model what is proven to work and adapt it. Test original ideas only after you have a working baseline to compare against.
- Small wording changes with everything else held constant can change results dramatically, which is why words are tested rather than admired.
- **Every communication ends with a call to action.** Judge a piece by the action taken, not by reach or praise.
- A piece of copy must be traceable to a result. An email with a link to a sales page can be measured in revenue; that is why link-driven formats are worth more than reach-driven ones.

## 11. AIDA: The Chain Every Piece Must Complete

```
A - Attention  --> pattern interrupt, hook, call-out
I - Interest   --> the problem, described so they recognise themselves
D - Desire     --> emotional pull plus logical case
A - Action     --> one clear call to action
```

It is sequential and each stage depends on the one before: no action without desire, no desire without interest, no interest without attention. A pattern interrupt is anything that breaks expectation enough to stop the reader (the classic example is a shirt ad whose model wears an eyepatch). A specific call-out of the target customer also works as attention, because the reader thinks "that's me".

## 12. Never Start From a Blank Page

- Always start from a structure and fill it in. Know every point you will hit before writing the first sentence.
- The structure does not have to be the same each time, and the opening does not have to be the same, but there is always one.
- Gather the raw material first: target customer, specific desired outcome, added benefit, biggest pain point, signature solution (see `copy-formulas.md`), then the problem layers, claims, dualities and angles (see `research-framework.md`).
- Whenever a good angle or line surfaces during research, note it immediately. A stocked list of angles is what removes "what do I write about next?"

## 13. Working Rules That Follow From the Principles

**Think problem, not product.** Approach a niche from the problem people have, not from the product being sold. The same message can carry different products as long as it hits the right problem. The product is the *vehicle* that delivers the solution.

**Lead with the problem, sell the solution before the product.** First say "you don't have to live with this, all you need is [the solution]" -- described as something to do, not something to buy. Only afterwards introduce the product as the best way to get that solution.

**Be specific.** "Lose 3 kg of belly fat" beats "lose weight". For most audiences there is one number or image that already stands for the goal in their head (for example a monthly income figure that means "freedom"); find it and use it. Specific enough that the reader says "that's me", not so narrow that almost nobody qualifies.

**Write to one person.** Picture a single individual, even give them a name and description, or pick a real customer who represents the audience. Use "you" wherever possible, even where a grammar teacher would insist on "they". One-to-one converts better than one-to-many.

**Repeat.** Repetition is the base of learning. A point made once usually does not land. Restate the desired outcome, the pain point and the promise several times in slightly different words.

**Name the solution so it cannot be compared.** A signature name avoids the category word. Calling something a "diet" invites comparison with every other diet; "protocol", "system", "challenge" places it in a category of one.

**Throw rocks at a shared enemy.** Distancing yourself from what the rest of the industry does ("I could tease you for 50 minutes like everyone else, but I respect your time") bonds you with the reader. A common enemy creates an ally.

**Take the weight off the reader.** "All you have to do is follow the steps" removes the need to think and decide. It moves responsibility from their shoulders to yours and lowers the perceived stakes of saying yes.

**Do not tell people they can't.** Prefer "doesn't feel like sticking to starvation diets" over "can't stick to a diet". The reader should feel understood, not diagnosed as incapable. Where possible, place the blame outside them.

**Handle objections before they are raised.** The biggest pain point of pursuing the goal is also the biggest objection. Put its removal in the promise ("without having to...").

**Hit more than one pain point.** An audience is made of different people who respond to different pains and benefits. After hammering the main one, touch others (no money, no time, no experience, not knowing where to start) so each reader finds theirs.

**Give reasons for claims.** "You won't have to invest money upfront" is good; the same claim with a reason why is better.

**Match the call to action to the commitment.** Low-priced offers can ask for the purchase directly from a short piece. High-priced offers ask for a smaller step (book a call, watch, register) and let the next asset do the selling. Email usually does not have to sell at all: its job is attention and the click to a page that sells.

**End positive.** Lead with pain if you like, but close on the way out.

## 14. Using AI the P2P Way

- The common mistake is asking AI to write the copy. Use it first for the *uncovering* work: put it in the prospect's shoes and have it produce the situations, feelings and conflicts you would not think of yourself.
- Brief it the way you would brief a 10-year-old or a new colleague: who the prospect is, what you are trying to find out and why, then the task. Teach it the concept you are using and give examples.
- Keep one continuous thread per project so every step builds on the last.
- Treat every output as a draft. Reject what is wrong and say why. You know the audience; the model does not automatically know that a "best friend's wedding" is rare and a weekend party is common.
- Do not use AI as a crutch for understanding. Doing part of the thinking yourself (perspectiving) is what makes you able to judge the output.
- Ideas are no longer the scarce skill. What separates strong copy is depth of audience understanding and emotional-level communication.

## 15. Language Adaptation

Always produce copy in the user's language. The principles are universal. When adapting, translate the *situation* and not just the words: pick daily-life scenes, numbers and expressions that are real for that audience.

## Quick Self-Check Before Delivering Any Copy

1. Does it open inside the reader's bubble (a recognised experience or held belief)?
2. Does it speak at the internal level, not just the external one?
3. Is there a clear duality the reader has to place themselves in?
4. Does each belief rest on the one before, with no skipped link?
5. Is the solution introduced before the product?
6. Would a 10-year-old understand every sentence? Would 100 readers get the same message?
7. Are the claims concrete (a scene, a number) rather than abstract?
8. Is there a logical reason to justify the emotional decision?
9. Is there exactly one clear call to action, sized to the commitment?
10. Does it end on the positive side of the duality?
